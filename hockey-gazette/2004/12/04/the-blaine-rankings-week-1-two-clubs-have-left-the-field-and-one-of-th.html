The Blaine Rankings, Week 1: Two clubs have left the field, and one of them is tearing through it

Toronto's ten-game winning streak has produced a goal differential no other club has matched all season, while Colorado sits one point ahead with a steadier hand. Everyone else is negotiating for relevance.

Howard BlaineSenior writer, The Blaine Rankings · The Hockey GazetteSaturday, 4 December 2004
ShareCopy link

There is a difference between being good and being a different kind of good. At the quarter-mark of this young league, we can name the difference by name: Colorado Avalanche is the first, and Toronto Maple Leafs is the second.

Colorado enters this week at 20-4-0, 42 points, the best record in the league by two points over Toronto and by five over anyone else. The Avalanche's dominance is architectural. Peter Forsberg89 has 52 points and the two-way game to justify a Hart conversation. Milan Hejduk83 has 49. Teemu Selanne81 has 44 on seventeen points of assists and twenty-seven goals. In net, Jamie Storr78 has won fourteen of eighteen with a 2.53 goals-against, and the team has allowed only 67 in twenty-five games — the fewest in the league by seven. Colorado wins because the structure is sound and the talent at the top of it is deep enough to absorb a bad night. They are 8-1-1 in their last ten, 50-21 on the goal ledger.

Toronto is something else. Ten consecutive victories, 75 goals for and 18 against across those games. The Maple Leafs sit at 18-5-0, 40 points, and they are not winning close — they are winning by four and five and six, repeatedly, in a way that makes the rest of the Northeast Division look like a different sport. Marian Gaborik95, still twenty-two, has 34 points at nearly nineteen minutes a night and the best overall grade (96) of any player under twenty-three in the league. Ed Belfour91, in ten starts, has nine wins, a 2.57 goals-against, and three shutouts. Over the full season, Toronto has scored 147 and allowed 76, and the bulk of that gap arrived during the current run.

The question I want logged is whether the standings at Christmas resemble the standings in April. On this evidence, the top of both conferences is already written. Colorado and Toronto are separated from the third-place clubs — New Jersey Devils and New York Rangers and Carolina Hurricanes — by a quality of play that no realistic hot stretch from those clubs can close in the time remaining. New Jersey is the exception worth watching: 13-2-3 in twenty-two games, Brodeur at 16 wins and a 2.48, and a payroll-to-point ratio ($1.22 million per point) that makes the other two look extravagant by comparison.

The Hart race is where the divergence between the two approaches becomes most instructive. Bill Guerin87 leads the league in points with 58, a 176-point pace over a full season, at thirty-four years old for Dallas Stars. Jaromir Jagr83 is second at 54 with a marginally better pace of 177, but Washington Capitals has 27 points and sits fourth in the Southeast. Peter Forsberg's 52 points on 171 pace for 42-point Colorado is, in my judgment, the more valuable body of work, and I will defend that position through April. Guerin's numbers are the best individual season in the sample; Forsberg's are the best season.

I log it: the Colorado Avalanche win the Presidents' Trophy. The Maple Leafs finish second. I expect to be revisiting this entry in the spring with either vindication or the embarrassment that comes with a broken streak.

Below the top two, the league is congested and ugly. The gap between third and tenth in the Eastern Conference is six points. The gap between Toronto and the next Northeast club — Ottawa Senators at 22 points — is eighteen. This is not a close race. It hasn't been for some time.
