Rick DiPietro on 21 Games, 7 Wins, and the Four Winters It Took

The first pick in 2000 talks through the season that broke him, the years he waited, and what comes after the last save.

Marcus BellRinkside reporter · The TunnelMonday, 24 October 2005

8:34 · synthetic voices, produced from the record · download

ShareCopy link

Rick DiPietro86 sat down with Marcus Bell in the studio after his fourth NHL season began with 4 wins and 2 losses in 6 starts for New York Islanders. The first pick of the 2000 draft, now 24, is the subject of the Sweater's profile "First Pick, Fourth Winter: the education of Rick DiPietro."

Transcript

Marcus Bell

Marcus Bell here, in the studio with Rick DiPietro of the New York Islanders. Rick, first time on this desk. Thanks for coming in.

Rick DiPietro

Yeah, good to be here, Marcus. Appreciate it.

Marcus Bell

Let's start with right now. Four and two in six, 14 goals against, you're on the ice 61 minutes a night. How is this different from the first 20, 25 games last year, your first real stretch?

Rick DiPietro

Yeah, I mean, last year I think I was still waiting for the other shoe to drop. Every game I thought, okay, they're gonna pull me, they're gonna decide I'm not the guy. And this year I just know what to expect from myself. I know my game. So when the puck's coming at me every night instead of once a month, I'm, you know, I'm not surprised by it anymore. I'm just playing.

Marcus Bell

Let's go back. You're the first pick in 2000. One year at Boston University, Hockey East Rookie of the Year, Beanpot MVP. You're supposed to walk in and take over. Your first NHL season: 21 games, 7 wins, 12 losses, 75 goals against. You're 19 years old. What was that like?

Rick DiPietro

I think I had no idea. I think I genuinely had no idea what I was getting into. You know, at BU you're having the best year of your life and everybody's telling you you're ready, and then you walk into the NHL and guys are just, they're better than you. They're 30, they've been doing this forever, and you're 19 and you're the first pick and every loss is, I mean, every loss is the story. And we weren't good. I was in there for 75 goals in 21 games. You carry that. You carry all of it when you're the guy in net.

Marcus Bell

And then the bouncing starts. Ten games the next season, call-ups, back down, ten games and a playoff game. How do you sit in that and not quit?

Rick DiPietro

I don't think I ever, I don't think quitting was the thing. I mean, obviously the results were bad, obviously I wasn't good enough yet. But I don't think I sat there thinking, this isn't for me. I think I sat there thinking, I need to be better and I'm not yet, and that's, that's a different feeling. Quitting is when you stop wanting it. I didn't stop wanting it. The waiting was just, you know, the waiting was the painful part. Watching guys get the call who were drafted way below you. You think, what do they have that I don't? And the answer is usually nothing. They just went first. But you can't let that turn ugly. You can't start hating your own room. Some guys do.

Marcus Bell

You've said the waiting made you patient. Did it make you better, though? Was there something you learned down there that you couldn't have learned up here?

Rick DiPietro

I think I got better at reading the game, yeah. When you're not playing, you're watching. And when you're in the minors you're watching guys who are grinding, who are not going anywhere, and they work every day. And you think, okay, I've been the golden boy since I was what, 14? And I've been kind of coasting on, you know, natural stuff. Agility, hands, speed. Those are the things that got me drafted. But those years made me a worker. I'm not a natural worker. I know that about myself. I like to be great. Those years taught me to be good first.

Marcus Bell

You just said you're not a natural worker.

Rick DiPietro

I am honest! That's the difference. No, but seriously, I like the result. I like the feeling of a good game. The process part, I had to learn that. That was the whole point of the minor-league years.

Marcus Bell

Let's talk about 2003-04. Your first full season, 63 games, 32 wins, 28 losses, a shutout. And then the playoffs, 5 games, 1 and 4. You get in and you don't get out. What did the playoffs show you that 63 games couldn't?

Rick DiPietro

That none of it matters. That's the harsh part. You go 32 and 28, you feel like okay, I've arrived, I'm the guy. And then you get into a series and guys are different. They're harder on you. They screen you different. They shoot pucks you never see in a regular season game because they don't dare in November. And I was one and four. We should have done more. I should have done more. You don't get a mulligan. You just get next year.

Marcus Bell

The Bureau's got you at 89 today, up nine on the Development Index from your base of 79, and your potential's 99. When you see those two numbers, the 89 and the 99, what do you see? A gap, or a path?

Rick DiPietro

I see the gap. I mean, the potential, everyone's got potential. You don't play potential. You play what's in front of you tonight. But the 89, I know where I was and I know where I am, and I think that's real. I'm not where I want to be. I'm probably, you know, some nights a guy's just bigger than me and he plants himself and I don't have the body for it yet. 185 at 6'1. You know?

Marcus Bell

Toughness and fighting are your two weakest areas. Fifty and fifty-one. You're a first pick with a 99 potential and a 50 in toughness. How does that sit?

Rick DiPietro

It sits fine. I know what I am. I'm not going to be a guy who takes a hit along the boards and likes it. That's not, that's not what I do. But I can get stronger in my crease. I can hold my ground. The playoff last year, there were pucks I saw late because a guy was just, you know, right in my face and I didn't push him off. And that's on me. That's not a size thing. That's a, I have to be willing. I have to decide it before the play gets there.

Marcus Bell

You spent the summer in the gym?

Rick DiPietro

Yeah. More than I used to. I was a hockey player who worked out before. I'm trying to become a guy who works out and then plays hockey. It's a small change in the sentence but it's a big change in how you spend the summer.

Marcus Bell

Let's talk about further out. You're 24, you've got zero years left on your contract, and by the Bureau's measure there might not be a better goaltending career in the building than yours ahead of you. But eventually the skates come off. Have you thought about what that looks like?

Rick DiPietro

Yeah. More than I probably should at 24. You're supposed to be in the middle of it, not looking ahead. But I see guys at 36, 37, and their body is gone. And I think, what am I going to be when I can't do this anymore? And honestly I don't know yet. I know what I don't want. I don't want to be on a stage selling something I don't believe in. That's the thing I'm sure of.

Marcus Bell

Goaltending coach. Is that the obvious one?

Rick DiPietro

I don't know. I've played 21 and 63 and this year's going, and the guys coaching in this league have played way more than that. I don't know if I'm qualified yet. But I like talking to guys, I like explaining things. So maybe. Or maybe it's something that has nothing to do with hockey. I've been kind of a new kid my whole life. New school, new team, new city. Maybe I'd like to find something where I'm not the new kid.

Marcus Bell

You were 10 when your family moved from Lewiston to Winthrop. What do you remember about that?

Rick DiPietro

The car ride. My mom was quiet the whole way and I didn't know why. You're 10, you don't get to decide anything. You just go. And I remember getting there and nobody knew who I was. And I think that made me okay with being the new guy. Which turns out, you know, is useful when you're the first pick and every year the room around you is different and you have to figure out where you fit.

Marcus Bell

You've got zero years left on $1.10M. You're walking. Is that a conversation you're having now or is it for later?

Rick DiPietro

It's on your mind. Obviously it's on your mind. It's your livelihood. But you can't play well thinking about the number. I learned that the hard way in those bouncing years. You think about the save in front of you and the rest takes care of itself. I'll let my guy do his job. I'll do mine.

Marcus Bell

One last one. You're 34, you're done, and you look back at a career that started with 7 wins in 21 games. What do you hope you've been?

Rick DiPietro

Consistent. I think that's the hardest thing. Anyone can have a good game, a good month. To be the guy who's always there, November through April, who your team can count on without thinking about it. I don't need to be the best. I want to be the one they don't have to worry about. That's, that's what I hope.

Marcus Bell

Rick DiPietro, first time on this desk. Thank you for your time.

Rick DiPietro

Thanks, Marcus. Appreciate it.
