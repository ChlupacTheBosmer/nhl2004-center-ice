Olaf Kolzig: "My father watches the clock, not the game"

The WSH goaltender talks about a life spent flying, a family still in Johannesburg, and why he matches up well against Jean-Sebastien Giguere.

Jan KowalczykRinkside reporter and studio host · The TunnelSunday, 4 December 2005

4:30 · synthetic voices, produced from the record · download

ShareCopy link

Olaf Kolzig89 sat down with Jan Kowalczyk in the studio after Washington Capitals practice. The 35-year-old goaltender is in his 16th season in North America, having left Johannesburg in 1989. The Bureau's Book has him at 89 overall, with a ceiling of 71, and the Development Index has him two below his base grade.

Transcript

Jan Kowalczyk

Jan Kowalczyk here with Olaf Kolzig. Olaf, thanks for sitting down with me. The Sweater ran a piece this week called Sons of Johannesburg, about your road and the fall the Bureau is projecting. You've been in North America since you were nineteen. What does that number feel like when someone says it back to you?

Olaf Kolzig

Yeah, sixteen years. I don't count it like that. I count it in games. You know, you don't think about it, you just wake up and you're here and the flight comes and you go. When someone says sixteen, I think, oh, yeah. My parents are back there and I'm over here. You know.

Jan Kowalczyk

You left at eighteen, I think, to go to Tri-City. How much was that decision yours and how much was the decision your family made for you?

Olaf Kolzig

Well, the decision was, I'll say, family. My father, he said, you go, you try, you come back if it's nothing. So I went. He didn't come to the first game. Not until, hmm, maybe 1998. The All-Star thing. That's when he said, okay, this is real. Before that I think he thought I was in college, you know, playing some hockey for fun.

Jan Kowalczyk

He came to the All-Star Game. Did he stay for the regular season after that?

Olaf Kolzig

No, no, no. That one trip. He flies back. You know, it's fourteen hours for him to come here. And then he has to go home to the shop. He can't leave the shop for a month. So he came once, watched me in the All-Star, and then he flew home and he was on the phone the next day saying, you played, I saw the, the TV from South Africa was good, you looked good, okay go back to work. You know.

Jan Kowalczyk

So he watches on TV? From Johannesburg?

Olaf Kolzig

Yeah, but it's, it's difficult. The time zone is so bad here, and he's in the shop in the morning. So he watches the clock more than the game, you know. He wants to know if you won, not how you won. If I let in four, he calls and says, you let four in, that's too many. And I say, yes. And then we talk about other things.

Jan Kowalczyk

You told me back in January that you play better on the road, that the building makes guys press. Is that still true now, at thirty-five, with a longer career behind you?

Olaf Kolzig

Yeah. Yeah it's true. On the road it's clean, you know, the crowd is against you but it's, it's simple. You know them by their jersey, they stand up, you see the puck. Here at home, the guys hold the puck too long, I see them turning with their backs to me, and I don't, I can't say anything because they don't hear me over their own crowd. You know. On the road it's quiet for me, almost.

Jan Kowalczyk

What does quiet mean at thirty-five though? Are the legs different on the second night of a back-to-back in a place like Anaheim?

Olaf Kolzig

Well, the legs are, the legs are fine. I think the mind is different. When I was twenty-five and I played seventy games, I just played seventy. Now I think about the sixty-eighth, the sixty-ninth, I think, can I still push. You know? But I'm doing seventy so maybe the thinking is not helping me, maybe I should just not think.

Jan Kowalczyk

The Bureau has your ceiling at 71 and your base at 92. That's a twenty-grade gap. Does that land when you hear it?

Olaf Kolzig

I hear it. I don't, I don't know what to do with it. They see me at 89 right now, down two from my base. Okay. And they think I'll be 71. I think, maybe. But I think I can still stop the puck. You know, when my father calls and says you let four in, that number means more to me than 71. You know.

Jan Kowalczyk

Let me shift. You won the Vezina last season. Giguere won the one before that, in Anaheim. Do you think of him as a rival?

Olaf Kolzig

Yeah, I do. Gaby is, he's good. You know, when we play them in Anaheim, I watch him on the screen in the tunnel, and he's always ready. Always in the crease early. He doesn't look around, he's just, he's just in there. And he's quick. The butterfly is, it's, his top is good, his bottom is good. I know that because I've scored on him... no, I mean, I've had pucks go by him. He's solid.

Jan Kowalczyk

What's the one thing about Giguere that you'd steal if you could?

Olaf Kolzig

His, his rest position. You know, before the puck comes, he's already, he's already square. I see him on video and he's not reaching, he's already there. I reach sometimes. That's maybe the, the consistency problem. If I was square like him from the start, maybe I don't give the second chance. You know.

Jan Kowalczyk

You've played against him in the playoffs. What's the difference between seeing him in October and seeing him in April?

Olaf Kolzig

Nothing, you know? That's the thing. That's why I respect him. When the building is full and everyone is loud and it's Game Seven, he's the same face. He's, he's not a man who gets excited and then gets cold. He's just there all the time. I'm not that. I'm the one that, you know, gets up and waves his arms after a big save. He doesn't do that. He just resets.

Jan Kowalczyk

You said your father calls when you let four in. Does he call after the Anaheim games?

Olaf Kolzig

Oh yeah. He calls. He says, you lost to the little goalie. That's what he calls Gaby. The little goalie. I say, Dad, he's a good goalie, and my father says, yes but you lost. You know? So it's always the result for him. Not the save percentage, not the, the grade, nothing. Just the win.

Jan Kowalczyk

Does he fly over again? You mentioned the fourteen hours.

Olaf Kolzig

Well, maybe for the playoffs. If we get there. That's the, that's the plan. If we get to the second week of the playoffs, maybe he comes. He's retired from the shop now, or close to it, so maybe it's easier. But I don't want to talk about the playoffs when we're in December. You know, one game at a time. That's what he teaches me. Not the, not the grade. Just the next game.

Jan Kowalczyk

Olaf Kolzig, thank you for your time. Appreciate it. Back to you in the studio.
