Sergei Fedorov on 61, Portland, and Wayne Gretzky's Kitchen

The 36-year-old center for the Mighty Ducks® of Anaheim Mighty Ducks tells Marcus Bell the potential number is for a boy who is eighteen, walks back to a Soviet hockey school in Pskov, and gives the two weeks he spent at Wayne Gretzky's house in Los Angeles the credit for the way he still plays at 22.2 minutes a night.

Marcus BellRinkside reporter · The TunnelThursday, 22 December 2005

8:02 · synthetic voices, produced from the record · download

ShareCopy link

Sergei Fedorov90 sat down with The Tunnel's Marcus Bell in the studio on December 22, three days after a 46-point season started its second month. He is 36 years old, 31 goals in 33 games for the Mighty Ducks of Anaheim Mighty Ducks, carrying a Book grade of 90 against a base of 94, a Development Index one point below his floor, and a potential the League Scouting Bureau has pinned at 61.

Transcript

Marcus Bell

Marcus Bell here in the studio with Sergei Fedorov of the Mighty Ducks® of Anaheim Mighty Ducks. Sergei, good to have you in the chair. Let's start with the number nobody in your building can stop talking about. The League Scouting Bureau has your potential at 61. Your base grade is 94. The Sweater ran a profile this week, Potential 61, Base 94: Sergei Fedorov at 36 and the Worst Number in the Book. Did you see it?

Sergei Fedorov

Yes, I see. Sixty-one. I see. Very nice. You know, when I am twenty-two, twenty-four, I read these things. My friend Slava, Vyacheslav, he check the Bureau every morning like newspaper. But now? Thirty-six years old. The sixty-one, it is for the boy who is eighteen. Who is playing in the junior and he do not know yet what he is. For me... it is a number on a page. I play on ice. That is different.

Marcus Bell

Does it bother you at all, knowing it's out there, that people are reading it?

Sergei Fedorov

In 1994, when I win Hart, nobody ask about potential. They ask, Sergei, what do you see on the ice. That is a good question. Now I am thirty-six, thirty-one goals in thirty-three games, and they say potential sixty-one. No, it does not bother me. I know what I can do. I know what I did. The sixty-one is... ...it is funny. That is all.

Marcus Bell

Let's go back to the beginning. You were born in Pskov. A small city. Where does a man from Pskov learn to play hockey?

Sergei Fedorov

Pskov. Yes. The winter is long. You are outside from five, six years old. The ice is not the good ice. It is the lake, sometimes, or it is what they pour in the yard behind the building. But you play. Of course you play. You do not choose it. Then the school take you. The hockey school. You are good, they say, come, you train morning and evening. Every day. A thousand times the same pass, the same pattern. And you do it faster. The game becomes like... ...like a dance. You feel where the man is going to be before the puck goes there. This is from Pskov. This is from the school. Nobody take this from you.

Marcus Bell

And then you made the Soviet national team. What did that add?

Sergei Fedorov

The national team, yes. They take you and the level is... very different. You play with the best boys in the country. And the coaches, they teach you the system. The passing, the skating, the head up, always head up. You see the man before he is open. In the Soviet team you do not have the option to be slow with your thinking. The game is very... ...it is very clean. Like mathematics. And I keep this. At thirty-six, my legs are not what they were, but the head, the head is here. The head is always here.

Marcus Bell

And the summer that changed everything. Portland, 1990. The Goodwill Games. You remember what it was like to make that decision?

Sergei Fedorov

Portland. Yes. I remember. Alexander Mogilny, he goes first. Then me and Volodya Konstantinov. The Red Wings, they have the media guide, a small book, and inside there is letter. They give it to us. You must understand... when you are twenty years old in the Soviet Union, you cannot just... it is not possible to leave. The door is closed. But the letter is in the book, and you read it, and you know. This is the life you wanted or the life you are given. You choose. I choose.

Marcus Bell

What surprised you when you got here? The first season in Detroit?

Sergei Fedorov

The players. So big. And they hit you... you know, in Russia you play the game. Here you play the game and you play the man. The puck is yours for one second and then a man is on you, on your back, on your ribs. I am seventy-three kilograms, I come here and the man is ninety. You learn very fast. The hands, you keep them close. The head, you keep it up. You learn or you go back home.

Marcus Bell

You mentioned once that Paul Coffey introduced you to Wayne Gretzky at the 1994 All-Star Game, and you stayed at his house in Los Angeles for two weeks. What was that actually like?

Sergei Fedorov

Wayne. Yes. Paul says, come, I want you to meet Wayne. And I meet the greatest player ever. And he is in his kitchen. He drink coffee. He talk about his children, about his garden. And the two weeks I watch him. I do not say so much. I watch how he move. How he see the ice. How he give the puck before I would give it. One step before. Two step before. And he is... he is a very simple man. The greatest player in history and he sit in the kitchen and talk about the garden. I keep what I see.

Marcus Bell

So Gretzky is the man who taught you the thing you still use? The one step before?

Sergei Fedorov

The Soviet school, they teach me the base. The passing, the skating, the feel for the ice. This is who I am from Pskov. From the lake. Wayne teach me the thinking. Where the play is going to be, not where it is. That is different. That is... ...that is the thing that keep you in the league when you are thirty-six. You cannot outrun the boy. But you can be where the boy is going to be one step before him. That is Wayne. That is the kitchen in Los Angeles.

Marcus Bell

Let's talk about your contract for a moment. You're on $6.30M. Some of the men your age, guys at the end of their deals, they talk about the noise it makes in the building, in the room. How do you keep it out of your head?

Sergei Fedorov

Three years. Marcus, I have three years. I am not in contract year. So the question is about a different man. But yes, you ask how. When you are twenty-five, twenty-eight, every game you think, this is the shift, this is the goal that make the number bigger on the paper. You play for the number. When you are thirty-six and the paper already says three year, $6.30M, you do not play for the paper. You play for the man in the net, for the man on your wing. The money is done. It is finished. You go home.

Marcus Bell

That sounds like a man at peace.

Sergei Fedorov

It is peace. The morale number, the Bureau says ninety-seven, and I think, yes. That is correct. Giguere is in the net, Slava is on the wing, we win together, we lose together, the money is on the paper. What else do you need from a hockey season?

Marcus Bell

You and Vyacheslav Kozlov are the two Russians in the room. He's got 32 assists in 32 games, which is the most in the league for a right wing. What's it like sharing the ice with a man from your country?

Sergei Fedorov

Slava. In Russia we say, two Russians in one room, it is too loud. But Slava is quiet. He pass the puck, that is his life. He does not need the goal. He give you the puck and you score and he is... satisfied. He smile. I like this. He is thirty-three and he know exactly what he can do and he do it every night. Many men cannot do this. They want to be someone they are not. Slava is Slava. That is why he has thirty-two assists.

Marcus Bell

22.2 minutes a night. You play center, you're on the penalty kill, you're on the power play. At 36, how is this game different from the one you played at 24?

Sergei Fedorov

At twenty-four I run to the puck. At thirty-six I am where the puck is going. The step is shorter. The thinking is longer. My speed, of course, it is not what it was in one thousand nine hundred and ninety-six, but I still have speed. I have the hands. The pass. These I keep. What I lose is the run. So I do not run. I go there before. That is the whole game at thirty-six. You arrive early. You do not arrive late and chase.

Marcus Bell

The Development Index has you at 90 today. Your base is 94. You're a point below. When you see that, what goes through your head?

Sergei Fedorov

A point. I play thirty-three games, the other team play thirty-two or thirty-four. The Index go up, go down, it is the weather. Today ninety, tomorrow ninety-one. I trust my legs. I trust my hands. My coach put me on the ice and I play. That is the only number I need.

Marcus Bell

You sound settled here. Is this where you want to play the last years?

Sergei Fedorov

I do not think about last years. I think about Thursday. I think about Saturday. I am here. Giguere is here. Slava is here. We have a team. Three years on the paper. We see. We see.

Marcus Bell

Sergei Fedorov of the Mighty Ducks® of Anaheim Mighty Ducks. Thank you for your time, Sergei. Good luck Thursday.

Sergei Fedorov

Thank you, Marcus. We must play sixty minutes.
