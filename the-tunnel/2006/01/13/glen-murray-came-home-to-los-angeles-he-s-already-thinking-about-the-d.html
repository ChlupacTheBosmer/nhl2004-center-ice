Glen Murray came home to Los Angeles. He's already thinking about the drive back to Halifax.

The 33-year-old right winger who won the Art Ross and the Lady Byng in the same season is back in a familiar building, at peace with where his numbers have gone, and mapping a second career with ice in it.

Marcus BellRinkside reporter · The TunnelFriday, 13 January 2006

Glen Murray Glen Murray86 returned to Los Angeles Kings on January 5, the same building where he spent five seasons before the 2001 trade that sent him to Boston Bruins. At 33, with 57 points in 43 games this season and 3 years and $7.15M a year left on his contract, he sat down with Marcus Bell in the studio after the morning skate.

Transcript

Marcus Bell

Marcus Bell here, studio, after the morning skate. Glen, you're eight days back in that locker room. How does it feel?

Glen Murray

You know, it's weird. Real weird. First time I was in this building I was, I don't know, young. Everything's been repainted. New weight room, new, new everything. But the ice is the ice. You step on it and your body knows where it is. And I, I know some of these guys already. Zig and I have been going at each other for, what, ten years? Good man to play with.

Marcus Bell

Jason Allison. The 2001 trade that sent you to Boston Bruins® brought him to Los Angeles Kings®. Five years later you're sharing a power play unit.

Glen Murray

Yeah. Me and Jase, we've talked about it. He says, 'You got me traded to the wrong city.' And I say, 'You got me traded to the right one.' So, one of us is always laughing. He still sees the game the same way. At center, he's got eyes in the back of his head. I'm out on the wall, I look over, and the puck's already gone somewhere I wasn't even looking at. It's a, it's a gift. Some guys just don't get that.

Marcus Bell

Let's talk about the number everybody brings up. 68 goals, 100 assists, 168 points in 82 games. Art Ross. Lady Byng. And you're at 57 in 43 this year. You know exactly where you are.

Glen Murray

Yeah. And I don't, I don't run from it. It was, it was that year. I was in the middle of everything. First line, PP, sometimes the PK. I was exhausted and it was the best I ever felt playing this game. And you can't bottle that. You know? You can't go back and say, 'Give me that February back.' It was a perfect storm of a guy who got hot and didn't cool off. And now I'm chasing fifty. Not a hundred. Fifty's, that's the goal now.

Marcus Bell

So what are you out there doing now, that year's behind you?

Glen Murray

I know what I am. I'm not, I'm not the guy that's gonna put up forty next year. I'm the guy that wins the battle, makes the play, gives Zig or Deadmarsh or Camm one more look. That's my game now. And you grieve it a little, you know? The first couple years when the legs aren't what they were. But after a while you just say, 'Okay. This is the game I play.' And that's fine.

Marcus Bell

Go back further than that. Halifax, Nova Scotia. Where does all this start?

Glen Murray

Halifax. God, yeah. My old man put me on skates when I was, I don't know, three, four years old. And you, you know how it is in Halifax. You've got these community rinks, the ones with the bad boards and the ice that's basically a frozen parking lot. And every kid within six blocks is out there from September to March. My dad, he'd get up at five a.m. to drive me to an early skate before his shift. Five a.m., every morning, all winter. I didn't think about it then. You think about it now.

Marcus Bell

Did he play?

Glen Murray

No! God, no. My dad couldn't skate. He was a hockey fan, which is worse. He'd grade me. Every game. 'You lost a battle along the boards, Glen.' I was nine. I'd just scored a shorthander and he's going, 'Well, yeah, but the second shift.' I thought he was out of his mind. Now? I think I'd be the same guy. If I had a kid playing, I'd be that annoying dad in the second row.

Marcus Bell

Eighteenth pick, first round, 1991. Did you see that coming?

Glen Murray

No! No, no, no. In Halifax in '91, you don't get a scout calling you on the phone going, 'Hey, you might go top twenty.' You get a newspaper. And my mother, she called me at my billet's house, and I think she said, 'I'm proud of you,' four times in ten seconds. And my old man, I walked into the kitchen and he was just, he was crying. He's not a crying man. And I thought, okay. That's the thing. Not the draft. The crying man.

Marcus Bell

You've got 3 years and $7.15M a year left. You're 33. When does 'after' stop being hypothetical?

Glen Murray

I think about it more than I should, maybe. Because I've seen guys, guys I played with, who get to the last day of the last year and they've got nothing. No plan. And I don't want to be sitting in a room at 36 going, 'I guess I'll figure it out.' So I've been thinking. And the answer keeps being Halifax. My old man's still there. My brothers are there. I want something there. A hockey school, a development thing. I don't know exactly what it looks like yet. But something with ice. With kids. Without the flights. You don't realize what the travel costs until you're thirty-something and every morning your body's telling you about it. You start counting the planes. How many more red-eyes to nowhere. And you start thinking, I want to drive to the rink. I want to park, walk in, be done by eight.

Marcus Bell

You've been sitting in that chair for a few minutes now. What about broadcasting?

Glen Murray

You're doing the heavy lifting, Marcus. I'm just sitting here. You ask, I answer. You want to see me with no question coming? I'd freeze. I'd just stare at you. But, yeah. Maybe. I like the, the chess part of it. 'Why did that line change, what does the coach see, where's the gap.' I like thinking about it. Maybe there's something in that.

Marcus Bell

Glen, thanks for your time.

Glen Murray

Thank you, Marcus. Good to be back in the building. Even if they did move the coffee machine.
