The Contract Year: Jarome Iginla plays for July

76 goals, 32.9% of his club's goals, 13 grades past his ceiling, 0 years of control left, and 60 days before free agency closes

Tomáš VránaProfile writer · The SweaterSunday, 29 May 2005
ShareCopy link

Jarome Iginla92 scored 76 goals this season for the Calgary Flames Flames, a club that won 22 games. His 76 accounted for 32.9% of Calgary's 229 total goals. He turns 28 on July 1, the same month his contract expires, and the Bureau's Development Index has him 13 grades past the ceiling they projected when he was a teenager in Kamloops.

The potential grade the Bureau carries for him is 81. His overall today is 94. The gap is 13 grades, and he has been playing above it for two seasons now. In 2003-04 he scored 92 goals in 73 games and won the Maurice Richard Trophy. This year: 76 goals in 76 games, a second Richard, and the Pearson Award as the league's best player as voted by his peers. The numbers went down. The team won 4 fewer games than last year. None of that is his fault, and all of it lands on him.

What the room looks like from the ice

Calgary Flames finished 22-43-8, 70 points, 277 goals against. The Flames sit second-worst in the league, ahead of only Montreal Canadiens at 66. Their second-leading scorer is Craig Conroy82 at 24 goals and 102 points. The gap between Iginla and Conroy is 26 points. The gap between him and the rest of the roster is wider than that.

He carries an A. His morale reads 92. He is not angry. The record does not show him asking for a trade, and the people around him would not say he is unhappy. But a man who scores 32.9% of his team's goals on a 22-win club, earning $6.50M with no years left, has already answered the question about whether he can carry this by himself. The answer is no. He is doing it anyway.

From St. Albert to the Nieuwendyk trade

Iginla grew up in St. Albert, a city north of Edmonton. His father, Elvis, came from Nigeria and became a lawyer. His mother, Susan, is from Oregon and worked as a massage therapist and a music teacher. His parents divorced when he was two. He was raised by his mother and his grandparents, and he credits his grandfather with making the hockey possible: with his mother working and his father in law school, nobody would have driven him to rinks at 5 in the morning.

Baseball came first. He was the catcher on the Canadian national junior baseball team and played until he was about 17, hoping to become a two-sport professional like Bo Jackson. On the ice, he played goaltender for his first two years of organized hockey because Grant Fuhr was the Oilers' goalie and Iginla wanted to be him. He switched to right wing at eight and never went back.

At 15 he led the Alberta Midget Hockey League with 87 points for the St. Albert Raiders. The Kamloops Blazers took him at 16. He won two Memorial Cups in three seasons: 1994, when he was 16 and had six goals and 29 points in 48 games, and 1995, when he scored 33 goals and 71 points and led the tournament with five goals. The Blazers expected to win every year. Iginla told the room what that felt like: putting on that jersey was like putting on the Canadiens'. You performed.

The Dallas Stars Dallas Stars took him 11th overall in 1995. On December 20, 1995, they traded him and Corey Millen to Calgary for the rights to Joe Nieuwendyk, who was in a contract dispute. Iginla was 18 years old. He has been a Flame ever since.

The Book and the Index

The Bureau's Book gives him 94 today, base 93, with the Index adding 1. His shot power reads 99. Speed 98. Balance 97. Heroism 96. Accuracy 95. Acceleration 94. Those are the numbers of a player the Bureau cannot project upward anymore, because the ceiling they wrote in his file is 81 and he broke through it three seasons ago. A man 13 grades past his ceiling has no room left on the page. The only numbers that can still move are the ones he puts in the box score.

The fighting grade reads 51. Breakaway speed 52. Durability 56. The Bureau does not see a fighter, a breakaway specialist, or a man built to absorb punishment. They see a 6-foot-3, 200-pound right winger who scores with his shot and his speed and his balance, and who has not missed a game on the injury record.

$6.50M and no years left

The contract carries $6.50M. Zero years remain. Free agency closes 2005-07-28, 60 days from today. The Calgary Flames Flames have the Bird rights, which means they can re-sign him above the ceiling. Whether they can afford to is a different question. A club that won 22 games, drew 14,567 a night at $79 a ticket, and generated revenue the Bureau's Contract Ledger does not make public, is not a club that wins bidding wars for the best player in the league unless that player wants to be there.

Morale reads 92. He has been in Calgary since he was 18. He plays an A. He told people once, about Kamloops, that the jersey meant you performed. That is not the statement of a man who is leaving. But a man who scores 76 goals on a team that wins 22 games has a right to want more, and the calendar gives him a door in 60 days.

The rookie draft is 10 days away. The Flames hold the 9th pick, which belongs to Tuomo Pihlman76 right now in Minneapolis. The door does not open until July. In between, the most talented player on the second-worst team in the league goes home to St. Albert, plays baseball with his cousins, and waits to see whether $6.50M and a 22-win team is enough to keep a man whose potential grade is 13 grades below what he has already done.
