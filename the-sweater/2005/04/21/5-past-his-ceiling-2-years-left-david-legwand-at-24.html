5 Past His Ceiling, 2 Years Left: David Legwand at 24

The 2nd pick from 1998 just scored 98 points on a team that lost in the first round. The Bureau published his potential three years ago. He walked through it this spring.

Tomáš VránaProfile writer · The SweaterThursday, 21 April 2005
ShareCopy link

The Bureau put David Legwand88's ceiling at 83. His base grade today reads 88. He is 24 years old, has two years left on $8.37M a season, and finished this campaign with 57 goals and 98 points in 82 games for a Nashville club that scored 253 goals in total. He owns 22.5 percent of them.

David Legwand did not grow up in Nashville. He grew up in Grosse Pointe, Michigan, a suburb east of Detroit where the Detroit Little Caesars program put two Pee-Wee teams in the Quebec International tournament in the mid-1990s and one of them carried this kid. Grosse Pointe North High School, then the Plymouth Whalers of the OHL, where he scored 54 goals and added 51 assists in the season before the draft. He won the Red Tilson Award as the league's most outstanding player. He was 17.

David Poile traded Nashville's first-round pick and second-round pick to San Jose in 1998 to move up to the second overall selection. The Sharks kept what they wanted lower down. Nashville got Legwand.

Then the mononucleosis hit in training camp. He went back to Plymouth for the full 1998-99 OHL season, missed what would have been his first winter in the league, and came back the following fall still building into his frame after a growth spurt. On December 23, 2000, in a game against the New York Rangers, he became the first player in league history to score on a penalty shot in overtime. He played for Team USA at the 2001 World Championship because Nashville did not make the field that spring.

Four seasons later, at 24, the Bureau's ceiling is 83 and he plays at a base of 88. That is 5 grades above what the Book published for him, and his form sits at +3 on the Index, 3 above even that base in live play. Morale reads 87.

What he is

The grades tell you where the production comes from. Speed 98. Agility 94. Acceleration 93. Those three numbers on a center who plays 22.2 minutes a night and shoots left. His deke is 91, his puck handling 91, his hero 93. The first thing people in the building notice, the thing you cannot coach into anyone, is the first step.

A teammate who has lined up beside him all winter, and who asked not to be named for this piece, said the hardest part about playing with Legwand is the second after the puck leaves your stick. "You make the pass and then for a half-second you genuinely do not know which direction the play is going, because he already has." The sentence carries no number. The number is 98.

The weakest marks: fighting 50, spin behind 55, ODBI 60. He does not finish checks into the boards on his own. He does not take off his gloves. Nashville asked for the goals and the center ice time, and he gives both in a package that asks its wingers to do the dirty work along the half-wall.

What he costs

Nashville's payroll this season sits at $46,773,440. Legwand takes $8,373,030 of it. That is 17.9 percent of the team's money, and he returned $85,439 per point.

The pace line gives the desk something to argue about. Last season, in 41 games, he scored 59 points. A pace of 118 points per 82. This season, over the full 82, he scored 98. The pace dropped 20 points. The raw production went up because he played 41 more games. Whether a 41-game sample at 118 was hot or a full 82 at 98 is his real number is the question every GM in the building is pricing into the summer his deal expires.

The Index has him at +3 right now, which means the Bureau thinks this spring's form reads above his already-elevated base. A man whose form rises while his pace drops is playing better hockey over a longer stretch, not getting worse.

The hip and the spring

His hip gave out sometime between April 4 and April 7. He was back on April 12. Nashville's first-round series against Mighty Ducks of Anaheim began April 8. He watched at least the opening two games from the room. The Predators lost the series 4-2, eliminated by Anaheim's fifth seed.

The playoff scoring page lists Scott Walker83 with 6 points, Martin Erat84 with 5, Vladimir Orszagh77 with 5, Martin Rucinsky78 with 4. Legwand is not on that list. Whatever he contributed came in small numbers, and the team did not find a way to win the four games it needed.

Everyone in the building knows what the opening games looked like without him. Walker finished with 83 points on the season in his own right, but the man beside him who scored 57 goals watched the first two playoff games in a sweater that was not wet.

What is left

Two years. $8.37M each year. The best teammate on the roster, Tomas Vokoun90, carries a 90 and is a goaltender. The best skater beside him is Walker at 83. Greg Johnson80 is 81. Oleg Tverdovsky80 80. Chris Gratton78 80. This is what stands beside a Speed-98 center.

Nashville finished with 101 points in the Central Division, second behind Detroit by 5. They scored 253 goals and allowed 226. The structure was there. The goaltending was there, at least until Vokoun got the ankle and Lasak could not close out the series. The depth behind the top two lines was not. Everybody in the building knows the reason: one of the best centers in the conference watched from the bench for the start of it.

The Bureau published a ceiling of 83 for a kid from Grosse Pointe who won the Red Tilson at 17 and missed his first pro camp with a virus. He is 24, he is at a base of 88, and his form reads 3 above that right now. The ceiling number, on the Bureau's own logic, is a prediction about a man's body, not a wall. Legwand went through it. Now the question is whether he can find enough around him to matter in May.
