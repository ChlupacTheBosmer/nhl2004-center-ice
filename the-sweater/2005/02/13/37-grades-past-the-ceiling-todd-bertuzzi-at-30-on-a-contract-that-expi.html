37 Grades Past the Ceiling: Todd Bertuzzi at 30, on a contract that expires in July

The Book has him falling 37 grades from here. He is scoring at a pace of 141, first in the Northwest, and his salary dies in five months.

Tomáš VránaProfile writer · The SweaterSunday, 13 February 2005
ShareCopy link

The Bureau's Book has Todd Bertuzzi89 at 95 today, two grades above his base of 94, which puts him on the Development Index at +2. His potential is 58. The gap is 37 grades, one of the widest in the league, and the Book expects him to fall further from here than almost any other man carrying a 95.

He is 30 years old, fifth in the league in scoring with 96 points in 56 games, a pace of 141 over 82. His contract pays $2.60M with 1 year left and dies in July. Vancouver sits first in the Northwest at 29-17-5, and Bertuzzi is the engine beside Markus Naslund85.

The road out of Sudbury

Bertuzzi grew up playing for two minor clubs in Sudbury, Ontario, before the Guelph Storm took him fifth overall in the 1991 OHL Priority Selection. Marc Crawford, then running the Cornwall Royals, passed on him. Crawford has said it was a maturity thing, that Bertuzzi was a big kid who had not grown into his body. The body caught up. By 1994-95 Bertuzzi scored 54 goals for Guelph, a single-season club record, and played on a tandem with Jeff O&#x27;Neill83 that led the league in scoring.

He went 23rd overall to the New York Islanders in 1993. Two summers later he still had no contract. General manager Don Maloney said he would use the second pick in the 1995 draft to select him again if the deal was not signed by July 7. At midnight, with the deadline pressing, Bertuzzi's agent Pat Morris closed a four-year, $4.6M contract. The agent had wanted Brett Lindros money, five years and $6.7M. He got four and $4.6.

A kid from Sudbury who had to be threatened with re-drafting to get a contract now sits fifth in the league in scoring and has a potential grade lower than most fourth-liners.

What the grades say

The Book shows a man whose hands are almost perfect and whose body is the liability. DEKG 99. PUCK 99. PASS 98. ACCU 96. BALA 96. Those are the five highest grades on his page, and they describe a player who protects the puck like a wall, passes with vision, finishes from range. HERO 96 rounds it out, the grade the Bureau uses for a player who makes the play when the game is on the line.

The bottom of the page tells a different story. FIGH 51. INJU 51. SPBI 53. The 51 in fighting means he will not win a hockey fight in a league that wants one from a 6-foot-5, 225-pound right winger. The 51 in injury resistance is what concerns people, because he is carrying 71 penalty minutes this season and a contract that expires in July. The 53 in speed means defenders know where he is going. He has made 96 points anyway.

The potential of 58 is the number that matters for July. The Bureau's Development Index carries him two grades above his base right now, which is the form term doing its job. But the ceiling, the long-term projection, says 58. A 37-grade fall. The man scoring at 141 a pace, whose hands are rated 99, the Bureau thinks has somewhere between four and six productive years left before the grades collapse.

The room and the money

His morale is 98, an alternate captain, skating 21.3 minutes a night. The team around him has Naslund at 91, Dan Cloutier82 at 88, Ed Jovanovski81 at 87, Trevor Linden79 at 5, Brendan Morrison78 at 84, Mattias Ohlund76 at 82. That is a roster built to win now, in a division where second-place Edmonton Oilers trails by zero points (73 apiece, though Vancouver Canucks holds the first tiebreaker at 29 wins to 22).

The cost per point on Bertuzzi this season is $0.027M. Naslund, who has 104 points and 56 games played, costs $0.042M per point. The gap between them is $0.015M per point, and it is there because Bertuzzi took less money than his hands are worth.

At 30, with 96 points and a Book that says 95, Bertuzzi is the most underpaid top-five scorer in the league. The trade deadline is 34 days away. The Bureau's ceiling of 58 means a team that trades for him is buying one year of 95-grade hockey, not the four-year window his hands would suggest. That is what makes the $2.60M so hard to turn down for a contender, and what makes it so hard for Vancouver to hold him past July.

What a teammate would say, in the register of a room where a man has been an alternate captain long enough that the young players watch how he ties his skates: he does not talk about the ceiling, does not talk about July. He skates 21 minutes and takes the puck to the net and leaves the money to his agent. A morale of 98 does not come from contentment with a contract. It comes from a man who knows what his hands are worth and has stopped telling people.

The fall, whenever it comes

The INJU 51 is the grade that will decide whether the 37-grade collapse is gradual or sudden. A man with speed at 53 and fighting at 51, playing 21 minutes against first-pairing defensemen, is going to be hit. He has been hit all season, 71 times, and the penalty minutes have not gone to the other side, a body absorbing punishment with no armor grade behind it.

Last season he played 63 games and scored 101 points. This season he is at 56 and 96, on pace for roughly the same number. The pace is 141 against 131 last year, so he is better than last year, at 30, on the year after the year the Hart Trophy conversation started for some people who watched 54 goals in 63 games.

The Bureau says it ends at 58. The Development Index has him at +2 right now, meaning the form term is still carrying him above his base. The day the form term goes flat or negative, he drops toward 94. The day the ceiling becomes relevant, he is a man playing for a minimum-salary contract with 58-grade hands, and the room around him has already moved on.

That day is not today. Today he is 95, first in the Northwest, and the $2.60M in his pocket is the cheapest top-five production in the league. The trade deadline is 34 days away. Free agency closes in 165 days, and nobody in the building is pretending they know what comes after.
