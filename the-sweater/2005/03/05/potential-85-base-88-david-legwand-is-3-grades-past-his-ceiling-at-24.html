Potential 85, Base 88: David Legwand is 3 grades past his ceiling at 24

The 2nd overall pick from 1998 was supposed to peak at 85. The Book says 88, and he is leading Nashville in scoring at 102.5 pace.

Tomáš VránaProfile writer · The SweaterSaturday, 5 March 2005
ShareCopy link

David Legwand89 learned hockey from the Detroit Little Caesars Pee-Wee teams, two winters in Quebec in 1993 and 1994, the same program that produced the kid who would become the fastest center in this league. Grosse Pointe North High School sat across the river from the rink where Little Caesars practised, and Legwand walked between them for four years before the Plymouth Whalers took him into the OHL.

He scored 54 goals and 51 assists in his draft year, won the Red Tilson Trophy as the most outstanding player in the Ontario league, and Nashville general manager David Poile traded his first-round pick and his second to San Jose to move from the first to the second overall.

Then the mononucleosis came. Training camp, the summer after the draft, and Legwand went back to Plymouth instead of staying for the season. The growth spurt that came with it meant the Predators told him to put on weight before 1999-2000. He was 18, sick, skinny, and back in junior while the club he belonged to played its second season of existence.

David Legwand is 24 now. The Bureau's Book has him at 87 today, which reads 3 above the Development Index because he is on a heater. His base is 88. His potential is 85. He has been 3 grades past what anyone projected for him since he turned 23.

He has 46 goals and 80 points in 64 games. That is 102.5 points per 82 games, the leading pace on a Nashville Predators club that sits 35-19-6, 84 points, first in the Central Division. He plays 22.5 minutes a night. His speed is 98, the highest rated center in the league. His agility is 93. His hero grade, the one the Bureau uses for players who make other players better in the moment, is 93.

The weakest number on his card is a fight grade of 50. A faceoff win of 55. An outside body check of 60. The Bureau built a skater who gets past you rather than through you, and at 74 inches and 190 pounds he is exactly what the drafting sheet said he would be in that respect. Everything else went higher than they thought.

The penalty shot

December 23, 2000. A game against the New York Rangers. Legwand was 20 years old, in his third NHL season, and he scored on a penalty shot in overtime. Nobody had done it before. The Predators lost that game anyway, missed the playoffs that year, and Legwand went to the IIHF World Championship with Team USA in the spring instead of a first-round series.

People reach for that anecdote. At 24 he is doing something harder than a penalty shot in overtime: he is the most important player on a team that has been the most stable club in this league since it drafted him. Nashville has not been below 84 points in the two full seasons the record covers. Last season they were 38-30, 100 points, 6th overall. This season they are one point behind Colorado Avalanche for the top seed in the Western Conference with 18 games to go.

Tomas Vokoun92 is 90 and stands behind him. Scott Walker84 at 82, Greg Johnson82 at 80, Martin Rucinsky81 at 79, Oleg Tverdovsky81 at 79. None of those men are carrying the team. His 80 points are the most on the roster by a wide margin. The second-highest point total among Nashville's forwards is Walker's 61 in 50 games.

What the room says

You do not find Legwand talking much in the corridor after games. Teammates say the quiet is not coldness. He came to Nashville at 18, sick and underweight, and stayed through seven years while the club built around him. His morale reads 94. He has 2 years and $8.37M left on his contract, so the question of whether he stays is not a question of this July.

"I went back to junior with mono," he said once, in the register of a man who has told the story enough times to strip it of bitterness. "I was 18. I thought that was the thing that happened to me. Turns out it was just the thing that happened before the thing that happened."

He means the penalty shot, the growth spurt, the first full season at 21, the 39-point year in 25 games as a rookie, the 79-point season at 22, the 61 at 23 cut short by whatever took 23 games from him. This year he has missed none. No injuries on record. No knee, no hand, no back, nothing on the injured list with his name on it.

The ceiling and what is past it

The Bureau's Development Index has him at +3. His base is 88. His potential is 85. That gap is not a rounding error. Three grades past potential means the man who drafted him to get the 2nd overall pick got a player the scouting report said would not exist. A GM looks at a potential of 85 and a base of 88 and asks whether there is anything left. The Index says his form is up. The pace says his production is the best of his career. His speed of 98 has not moved; the Book grades the body before the game catches up.

He is 3 years older than Ilya Kovalchuk86, who sits 4 grades above him in the Book at 92. He is 4 years younger than Sergei Fedorov86, who is 33 grades past his ceiling and falling. Legwand is in the window where past-ceiling players either hold or break. At 24, with no injuries on record and a morale of 94, the room around him in Nashville thinks he holds.

Nashville plays the next 18 games with a trade deadline 14 days away. Poile has not called about Legwand, and no club would have reason to ask. A 24-year-old center with 80 points and 2 years left at $8.37M is not the kind of asset a club moves in March unless it is rebuilding, and Nashville is one point from the top seed in the West.

The Little Caesars Pee-Wee kid from Grosse Pointe is the best center on a Presidents' Trophy-caliber team, and the Bureau still has his ceiling at 85. The Book says 88. The pace says 102.5. He is 24, healthy, and nobody in Music City has a correction to offer him.
