First Overall, Third Option: Patrik Stefan plays for July at 24

The 1999 first pick has speed the Book rates at 95 and a contract that expires in six months, on a room built around two men drafted after him.

Tomáš VránaProfile writer · The SweaterTuesday, 25 January 2005
ShareCopy link

Patrik Stefan81 was the first name called in the 1999 draft. He is 24 years old, he earns $1.10M, and that money is up in July. The Atlanta Thrashers have won 15 games in 48. He has 37 points. The two men who make this room go are Dany Heatley90, taken second in 2000, and Ilya Kovalchuk92, taken first in 2001.

Heatley has 80 points in 45 games. Kovalchuk has 87 in 48. Stefan has 37 in 48. The Book grades Kovalchuk at 92, Heatley at 90, Stefan at 81. The man drafted first has the lowest grade of the three and the fewest points by a wide margin. The Development Index has him up 2 this edition, but the distance he has to close is 9 points of grade and 43 points on the scoresheet.

What the Book does say about him is worth reading slowly. His speed is 95. His shot power is 91. His agility is 88, his endurance is 88, his acceleration is 86, his aggression is 86. Those are first-line numbers on the physical and skill side. Then the bottom drops out: checking 57, toughness 55, fighting 50. A player with 95 speed and 57 checking is a man the coach lines up for 18.6 minutes a night, which is where the record has him this season.

The potential is 87. The overall is 81. The Bureau sees 6 more points of growth left in him. At 24, that is the last reasonable window. The players the Bureau has at potential 87 or above who are already at or near that number are the ones teams build around. Stefan is the one where the ceiling is in sight and the floor, on this team, looks close to the ceiling.

The contract and the market

$1.10M, zero years remaining. He hits the open market in July. The question the room cannot answer is what a team pays a 24-year-old center with 95 speed, 37 points, and a 57 in checking. The Bureau's Book has him at 81 overall. That is a good player. It is not a first-pick player, and the first-pick price has long since reset for a man at this grade on a 15-win team.

Stefan came from Atlanta Thrashers before the record opened in December. He was already there when the bookkeeping started. The arrival is older than the record. What the record shows is a man who has played 48 of 48 games, taken 8 penalties, and logged 18.6 minutes a shift. He is available and healthy, the third man on the depth chart of a team that finished last in the Southeast last year and sits fourth this year.

What the room looks like from the third seat

Heatley and Stefan are the same age. Kovalchuk is three years younger and 11 points above Heatley and 50 points above Stefan. The Atlanta Thrashers front office has two men it can build around and one it is trying to figure out how to pay. The two men it can build around were not the first name called in their drafts. Heatley was second. Kovalchuk was first, but four years later. Stefan holds the original title.

In Pribram, Czech Republic, in 1980, none of this was visible. The record carries his birthplace, his height (6 feet, 1 inch), his weight (205 pounds), his left shot, and the year the league drafted him. It does not carry the road between a boy in central Bohemia and the first name on a draft card in a North American arena. That road is his.

The shape of a third option

The grades that hold him back are the ones nobody drafts for. A player with 50 in fighting and 57 in checking will not win a puck battle along the half-wall against a man with 80 in both. He will not clear a crease. He will not finish a check. The 95 speed means he gets open, and the 91 shot power means he can score once he does. The problem is the 47 other minutes a game where the puck is in a corner and someone has to put their shoulder into another man.

The Development Index has him at +2. His morale is 92. He plays every game. He takes 8 penalties in 48 games, which is about as clean as a center gets. He is not sulking. He is a good hockey player in a room that has two very good hockey players who happen to be better in every way the record measures.

What happens in July depends on what another team sees. A club with cap room and a need for a second-line center who can skate might look at 95 speed and 91 shot power and think they can fix the 57. The Atlanta Thrashers might look at $1.10M and potential 87 and think they have one more year of development left in him before they let the market test it. The record gives no answer. It gives a dollar figure, a date in July, and a player who is the first name on a list of achievements his team has already moved past.

He played 18.6 minutes on a night in January with two men beside him who together put up 167 points. The record does not say how that felt. The morale of 92 says something, but it does not say everything.
