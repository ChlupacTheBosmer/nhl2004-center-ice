Seventh Overall, Zero Years Left: what Speed 93 costs at $1.10M

Mike Komisarek scored 82 points as a defenseman at 22, plays 29.5 minutes a night on the worst team in the Northeast, and his contract ran out in September.

Tomáš VránaProfile writer · The SweaterSunday, 6 November 2005
ShareCopy link

The Bureau's Development Index has Mike Komisarek81 up 10, which puts him at 80 today against a base grade of 77 and a potential the Book sets at 90. He is 23 years old. His contract reads $1.10M with 0 years left, which means he is a free agent in waiting, playing 29.5 minutes a night in Montreal, a city where 0 years on a contract and last place in the Northeast division tend to arrive at the same press conference.

Komisarek came from West Islip, New York, a village on the south shore of Long Island. Suffolk PAL, then St. Anthony's High School on two years of varsity, then the New England Jr. Coyotes in the EJHL under Gary Dineen, then the USA Hockey National Team Development Program, then two seasons at the University of Michigan. Forty-six points and 145 penalty minutes in 80 games for the Wolverines. A CCHA title. Two trips to the NCAA tournament. A First Team Division I All-American nod in 2002. The Canadiens took him seventh overall that summer. They signed him on July 24, 2002, and he dressed for his first NHL game that fall.

His first full season produced 24 games, 3 points, and 11.8 minutes a night, a call-up who saw enough to understand what he needed to fix. Then 2003-04: 82 games, 15 goals, 67 assists, 82 points, 30.4 minutes a night. A defenseman who finished tied with his own age in points. The Norris went to Bryan McCabe84 in Toronto, but Komisarek's line sits on the sheet like a man who just found out what the league asks for and answered it in his second year.

This season he has 12 points in 13 games, 11 of them assists, at 29.5 minutes a night. Montreal is 3-5-3 with 9 points, last in the Northeast, outscored 23-30. He is playing a minute less than his league-leading 30.4 from last year on a team that needs every one of them. The penalty clock reads 46 minutes in 13 games, up from 40 in 82 last year. His Checking grade is 87, his Aggression grade 82. He is playing hurt-adjacent minutes, the kind that keep a coach from pulling a defenseman even when the scoreboard runs against him.

What the Book says about the body

The attribute grades tell you why a 6'5" 225-pound right-shot defenseman from Long Island was a lottery pick in 2001. Speed 93. Slap Power 91. Acceleration 88. Checking 87. Agility 84. Aggression 82. Those six sit in a row on his card and the top four are all above 87. The only man guarding him would need a Fighting grade above his 51, Puck Handling near his own 80, and the patience to chase a Speed 93 skater into the neutral zone. Most clubs have two of three. The League Scouting Bureau's Book puts his potential at 90, which means the Index has him 10 grades below his ceiling and climbing.

The floor is thin too. Faceoffs at 54 for a defenseman is not a problem. Fighting at 51 means he is not the guy dropping the gloves when a winger comes across the line. Those are the trade-offs a man accepts when he picks 93 speed over everything else.

His morale reads 89. On a team that has lost two in a row and sits 9 points after 13 games, that number is the most interesting line on his card. A man with 0 years left on his contract and 10 grades between his current grade and his potential, in a locker room that has allowed 30 goals in 11 games, still reads high.

What the room would say

Komisarek grew up in a village where the nearest rink was a drive, played varsity hockey at a school that produced no NHL players before him, and went from EJHL to NTDP to Michigan in four years without skipping a step. Gary Dineen coached him in New England. Aleksey Nikiforov, the Lithuanian coach known for developing defensemen, had him at the NTDP. Nobody handed him anything at West Islip or at St. Anthony's, and the two years at Michigan produced a First Team All-American season and a CCHA title before he crossed the border.

A man who walks that path and finds himself playing 29.5 minutes on a team that cannot win at home (2-4) or on the road (1-4) does not look at the standings the way a 23-year-old with 13 years of team success might. He looks at his own minutes, his own points per game. Eleven of his 12 points are assists, which means he is moving the puck and watching wingers miss the net. The assist total in 13 games is within two of his goal total in all of 2003-04. He is making plays at the same rate.

July is coming

$1.10M with 0 years left. The Contract Ledger has him at a cap hit the league would call a rounding error for a top-pairing defenseman with 82 points in a season and 12 in 13. His potential is 90. The Index has him at 80. That gap is what every general manager in this league is pricing right now, and the price will not be $1.10M.

Montreal finished 21-46-66 last season, the worst record in the league. They are 3-5-3 again. The roster around him has Jose Theodore87 at 87 in net, Dwayne Roloson82 at 82 as the backup, Richard Zednik81 at 80 on the wing, and a second line center in Michael Nylander77 at 76. Komisarek is the third-highest-graded player on the club and the youngest man in the top five. He has been in Montreal since before the record opened, which means he has been here since at least the fall of 2004, and the building has not improved.

A man whose contract is up plays differently by January, but not in a way that shows on a stat line. It means he takes the hit along the boards, keeps his head up after the open-ice check, and plays 29.5 minutes because the coach asks and the next contract pays by the minute. His 46 penalty minutes in 13 games suggest he is not dodging contact. The Index is up 10 and the morale is 89. He believes this is the year the number jumps to where the Bureau says it can go.

Sometime before July, a general manager in a city with salary cap room gets a phone call. "Komisarek, 12 in 13, $1.10M." They read the line out loud and the room shifts.
