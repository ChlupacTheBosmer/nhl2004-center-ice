Letters to the editor

The Postbag: one question the schedule has not reached, one fan who may not want this answer, and one penalty the Bureau already printed

Three letters from a two-game season. A Sabres supporter asks about a road split that does not exist. A Rangers man gets the profit line. An Anaheim woman learns the number is on the Bureau's page.

Thursday, 13 October 2005 · edited by Tunde Oyelaran

Two games in, and the mail already carries three questions that need the record more than the eye. A Sabres woman wants to know why her team looks different on the road. A Rangers man wants to know what $92.95M is buying. An Anaheim supporter wants to know if her building is real or if it is something people say. One of the three has no answer yet because the schedule has not reached it. The other two are on the sheet.

We are a different team on the road and nobody will say why. Is it the travel, or is it an excuse?

Halina Sobczyk, Cheektowaga

You have played 1 game, Halina. You won it, 5-3, at home. Buffalo Sabres carries a road record of 0-0 because the club has not yet left the building this season. There is no road split to explain because there is no road sample. Your first road game is tonight, at Chicago Blackhawks. Come back after a handful of road dates and write again. If the differential holds, the Bureau's buildings and travel pages will be the first place I look.

We pay more than anybody and I would like somebody to tell me what for.

Aaron Lindqvist, Yonkers

The payroll is $92.95M, the largest in the league. The business table prices it at $23.24M a point on the 4 points you have earned through 2 games. I understand the arithmetic behind the question and what it feels like to read a payroll figure that large and then look at a goal differential of +2. New York Rangers is 2-0-0, second in the East conference, 6 goals for and 4 against. Revenue stands at $31.23M. Profit reads $21.41M. A seat costs $82, and 13,831 people filled one on the only home date so far. You have the league's largest payroll and you are turning money on it.

Alexei Kovalev90 has 3 points in 2 games on 25.5 minutes a night, carrying an 86 overall. Pavel Bure88 has 2 goals. Petr Nedved88 has 2 goals. The men on that payroll are producing through the first two nights. You asked what the money is for. Two wins, a conference seat, and a profit line of $21.41M are the answer the sheet gives tonight. If it goes sideways by December, write back and we will run the same arithmetic on a longer line.

Does the state of this building actually cost us anything on the ice, or is that something people say?

Delphine Okonkwo, Anaheim

No, it is not something people say. The Bureau carries a facilities number on every club's published grades, and Mighty Ducks of Anaheim sits at -3.65, the worst in the league. That comes off every man's overall. It applies across the entire roster. Every club's number sits next to yours on the same page for anyone to read, and the buildings page has the workout facilities and the travel arrangements that produce it.

Your club is 1-1-0 through 2 games, 4 goals for and 4 against, in a Pacific division that has not separated yet. The penalty is there whether the wins come or not.

Montreal's Stefaan Bullard, talking to The Tunnel's Marcus Bell after Monday's overtime loss, said what a building costs from the inside: "I lost games in February. I lost games where we were the team that got run over in the third period. And the workout room, that's where you build the strength, the checking, the endurance." He went further on the road: "We spend half the year on a plane, and right now we're taking that penalty every road trip. Every single one. I know what it costs us. I know exactly what it costs us." One coach's account of one building, on his own road trips. Your number is yours. The Bureau printed it in the summer edition and it has not moved.

---

Remaining patterns: None significant. The "No, it is not something people say" contrast is kept because the correspondent directly posed the alternative in her letter. The triad in the Bullard quote ("strength, the checking, the endurance") lives inside quotation marks and is the speaker's own rhythm.
