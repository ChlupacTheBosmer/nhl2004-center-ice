What the Book Never Said: Richard Park, 14 Goals in 16 Games, and a Potential of 75

He was gone from this league for five years. Now at 29, the Wild's quiet right wing has matched his career goal total in half a season, and the Bureau's grades say he should not be able to.

Carol BinghamFeatures writer · RinksideSunday, 13 November 2005
ShareCopy link

Richard Park79 was born in Seoul on a Thursday in May 1976. At 13 he crossed an ocean and ended up in the Greater Toronto Hockey League, wearing a Toronto Young Nationals sweater at the Quebec International Pee-Wee Tournament in 1990. By 16 he was at Belleville in the OHL, three seasons of junior hockey on a rink in eastern Ontario, small and fast and shooting right. The Pittsburgh Penguins picked him 50th overall in 1994. He played one NHL game that season and 56 the next. Then he was gone.

Five years. No roster, no call-up, no box score. When Park came back in 2001-02, it was to the Minnesota Wild on a try and stayed. Three seasons in Minnesota brought him a career high of 81 games played, 14 goals, 15 assists, 25 points. He scored the overtime winner in Game 6 against Colorado Avalanche in the 2003 playoffs, the kind of goal that gets replayed for a week and then filed.

This season, at 29, he has 14 goals in 16 games. He has matched his entire previous career high in goals before the November ice is gone. Twenty-three points put him third in this league in scoring, behind only Markus Naslund91 and Todd Bertuzzi93. His pace reads 118, which over 82 games is a 97-point season, numbers that would carry his name into conversations about Norris voters in spring.

The Book said 75

The Bureau's Development Index does not agree with the scoreboard. The Book's base grade for Park is 82. His current overall is 77. His potential grade is 75. That is the number the scouting network prints when they say a man will not get better. The Development Index has him 1 below his base, not a riser, not on any list the Bureau publishes for players whose trajectory points up.

The grades that are high on his card are the ones that score goals. Speed 88. Shot Power 86. Puck Control 84. Acceleration 83. Accuracy 83. Deking 81. The ones that are low tell you why the Bureau stopped believing: Checking 50, Fighting 50, Injury 50. He cannot take a hit. He cannot give one. The Book reads his body as fragile and his frame as insufficient, and it built a ceiling from those numbers and forgot about the ones that put the puck in the net.

What five years away does to a career

Park played 57 NHL games for Pittsburgh in two seasons, 1994-95 and 1995-96, and was 19 years old when he last wore that sweater. By the time he signed with Minnesota Wild in 2001 he was 25, which in hockey terms is a man whose development window is closing. The background does not say what he played between 1996 and 2001. The record does not know either. What it knows is that when he came back he was not the same prospect, not by grade or by expectation.

He was good enough for Minnesota to keep. Three seasons of 14-goal highs told the Wild that he was a 10th or 12th forward, the man who fills a wing when someone is hurt. His contract reflects that: $2.01M, 2 years left on the sheet. Morale 92.

The room he is carrying

Minnesota Wild sits at 4-7-3 through 14 games, 15 points, 43 goals for and 44 against. The club's payroll is $51.77M and the per-point cost reads $3.45M. They are in the middle of this league by every measure except the one that matters in November: they are not winning.

Their captain, Saku Koivu86, is out with a shoulder, designated by the league sometime between October 29 and November 6, back November 29. Until then the center ice belongs to Wes Walz84 and Pierre-Marc Bouchard81, and the production that would normally run through Koivu's line runs through Park instead. His 23.8 minutes a night is the highest of his career, the number the coach gave him because the room needs someone to score and he is the one scoring.

Manny Fernandez92 guards the net at 88 overall, the best player on this roster by grade, and the goaltending has been decent: 2.18 goals against and a save percentage of 0.866 in 11 starts. That is not the problem. The problem is that a 4-7-3 team cannot score enough to give its goaltender a cushion, and for three weeks of that drought, Park has been the only Wild forward with any resemblance to a consistent threat.

The table is the whole argument. Sixteen games has brought him to the same goal total that took him 81 games to reach before. His assists are lower, and his point pace is below what it was last season (90 points over 82 games then, 118 pace now on goals alone). The production has not spread; it has concentrated. He is scoring goals at a rate he has never scored them, and no one in this league's scouting network predicted it. Potential 75.

Two years on the clock

The contract reads $2.01M with 2 years left, which puts him under team control through 2006-07. If the pace of 118 holds, if he finishes with 40 or 50 goals, if he makes this team relevant in a playoff race, the renewal question will land on Minnesota Wild's front office in 2006 and the number will not be $2.01M. Morale is 92. He is happy in the room. That number will matter when the phone rings in July.

For now he is 29 years old in Minnesota, in his sixth NHL season, on a team that plays to November 29 waiting for its captain to heal, and he is the best player on the roster by production. The Book has said 75 for some time. Richard Park has 14 goals in 16 games and a Shot Power of 86 and a Speed of 88, and none of those numbers ask the Book's permission.
