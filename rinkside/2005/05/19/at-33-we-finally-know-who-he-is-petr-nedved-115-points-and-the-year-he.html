At 33, We Finally Know Who He Is: Petr Nedved, 115 Points, and the Year He Gave Up

The second pick of 1990 missed his age-26 season entirely. Fifteen years later, he leads the Rangers' scoring and carries the cheapest contract that matters on that roster.

Carol BinghamFeatures writer · RinksideThursday, 19 May 2005
ShareCopy link

Petr Nedved86 scored 115 points this season. No other New York Rangers player finished in the league's top 30. He is 33 years old. He makes $4.60M. He has 2 years left on the deal.

The Rangers spent $78.14M this season and finished with 84 points. Their cost per point, according to the Bureau's Contract Ledger, is $0.93M, the worst ratio in the league. The one man on that payroll who delivered at full rate did so on a contract the front office signed in 2003, back when he was still considered damaged goods, and nobody has tried to move since.

Nedved was the second pick of the 1990 draft, ahead of Jaromir Jagr84 at fifth. The Canucks took him after 145 points in 71 games for Seattle in the WHL. He looked, at 18, like the best pure scorer in the draft. By his third season he had a club record of 15 straight games with a point, 38 goals, 71 total. Then he held out, went to the 1994 Olympics for Canada, came back, and got shipped to New York. Pittsburgh took him the next year.

The year that broke him was 1997-98. He was 25. He had 33 goals and 71 points the season before. He was in his prime, or the shape of it, and he sat the whole thing out over money. Not a trade, not an injury. A contract argument. He went back to the Czech Republic and played lower-level hockey while the league carried on without him. The year after that he was in the IHL.

The record does not carry what he might have done with that year. The Bureau's base grade is 88 today. At 25, with a ceiling somewhere above what he is now, the assumption was that the second pick of 1990 would still turn into something. He missed it. He spent the next five years rebuilding a reputation that the league, generously, had not forgotten.

His return was slow. The background lists 32 goals and 78 points in 2000-01, on a line in Florida the newspapers called the Czech Mates, and marks it as the second-best season of his career. After that, the numbers plateaued. He drifted through clubs. The record opened with him already at the Rangers, and nobody has mentioned him in trade talk since.

This year he played all 82 games for the second season in a row. 51 goals, 64 assists, 115 points, 21.3 minutes a night. The Development Index has him at zero, which means his current form matches his base grade exactly. The Book says he is 88 today and was 88 at the start of the season and will not become 89. He is, at last, the player the Book graded him to be.

Beyond the nostalgia of a 1990 draft class, this is about the money. The Rangers' payroll is $78.14M, the second-highest in the league. Their 84 points bought them nothing: they watched the playoffs from home. The 8 players on this roster rated 85 or above include Alexei Kovalev88 at 88, Pavel Bure86 at 87, Eric Lindros87 at 87. None of them finished in the league's top 30 in scoring. Nedved, at 86, did, with 115 points.

The contract has 2 years left at $4.60M. In a league where Chris Pronger86 earns $9.50M for 43 points and Mario Lemieux88, at 39, earns his money by existing, Nedved's $4.60M for 115 points is a number the Rangers will never get back if they let it expire.

He has no trophies. The injury list is blank. His endurance grade is 90, his speed is 90, his agility is 90. He does not hit, he does not fight, and the Book gives him a 50 in that department. None of it has mattered. He has been on the ice 82 games in each of the last two seasons and produced the points his grade says he should.

At 33, Petr Nedved has played 15 seasons since the draft, missed one of them by choice, and is finally producing at the rate the second pick implies. The Rangers will have him for two more years. Whether they build around him, or simply carry him until the contract runs out while they try to figure out what to do with the rest of that payroll, is the question that comes after this season ends. For now, the arc is this: the man who threw away a year at 25 has, at 33, become the thing they drafted.
