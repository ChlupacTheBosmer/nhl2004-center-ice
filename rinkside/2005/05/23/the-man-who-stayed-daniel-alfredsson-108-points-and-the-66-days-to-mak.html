The Man Who Stayed: Daniel Alfredsson, 108 Points, and the 66 Days to Make It Worth the Stay

He demanded a trade at 25 because a teammate made $1M more. He never left. Now at 32, with his career-best season finished and no contract, Ottawa has two months to decide what staying was worth.

Carol BinghamFeatures writer · RinksideMonday, 23 May 2005
ShareCopy link

The 133rd pick in the 1994 draft came from a city nobody at Ottawa's scouting department had mapped. Gothenburg, Sweden, a kid with a right-handed shot and no pedigree the Bureau would have recognized. Daniel Alfredsson89 showed up at the Senators' training camp in 1995 and won the Calder Trophy by the spring of 1996.

Nine years later, on a late August morning in 1997, he asked to be traded. The contract in front of him was $1M a year less than what the club had paid Alexei Yashin. He was 25. He was already the captain. The room had not decided which of those two things mattered more, and Alfredsson wanted to know.

He stayed. That is the story of Daniel Alfredsson in Ottawa, and the Senators are about to find out what it costs.

This season he scored 56 goals and 52 assists for 108 points in 81 games. His previous career best, according to the public record, was 80 points in 78 games the year before. He scored more goals than he ever had and played 21.5 minutes a night at an age when most wingers are being asked to give a line back.

His overall on the Bureau's Book is 90. His strength grades tell you how he does it: a 96 deke, a 95 pass, a 94 shot and a 94 speed. The 93 on his hero rating is the number that matters most to Ottawa. Alfredsson carries rooms. You can see it in the line the Senators finished with, a 77-point club that scored 254 goals and let in 279 and missed the postseason by the width of a single division.

The room he built and the one that couldn't hold him

At 32, on the third-cheapest contract among the ten best players on that roster, Alfredsson was the highest-rated man the Senators had. Marian Hossa84 finished with 121 points and an 87 overall. Martin Havlat88 scored 103. Neither of them is the captain. Neither of them was the one who walked into a general manager's office in 1997 and said the number on the table was an insult.

The 28-point leap is not a hot streak. The Bureau's Development Index lists him at flat, and his base and current grade agree at 90. It was him, at 32, playing the best hockey of his life on a team that went 28-39-77.

And now the contract is gone. Zero years left. $4.70M on a roster that spent $42.80M for 77 points, which works out to $0.56M a point and the sixth-best ratio in the league. They booked $32.36M of profit on $82.74M of revenue, with 14731 a game at $80 a ticket. The money is there; the question is whether it was ever the point.

What staying cost him, and what it might cost them

In the 1997-98 season, Alfredsson missed the opening weeks while the contract dispute played out. He came back, signed, and finished the year as the same player who had been an All-Star the February before. The club kept him. He kept them. For eight seasons, that arrangement looked like loyalty. Now, at 32, with 108 points in the record and a free-agent flag blinking on the Bureau's ledger, it looks like a bargain about to expire.

He has a 53 on his injury resistance and a 50 on his fight grade. He is not the kind of winger who breaks through you in the neutral zone or drops the gloves to change the momentum of a game. He is a 96 deke and a 95 pass, a man who wins 20.1 minutes a night by being somewhere other than where you expected him to be. At 32, with an INJU of 53, the window that looks like this season is not guaranteed a look next season. His hip cost him a handful of games in late March and early April, back in the lineup by 2005-04-07, but the window narrows from here.

Alfredsson has 66 days before free agency closes. He can stay on $4.70M and play for a 77-point club that made the playoffs one round in five years, or go to a building that will pay a captain's salary to a man who has never won a playoff series in a seven-game set. The record from the public file carries it: Ottawa lost to Toronto in four in 2001, in seven in 2002, in seven in 2004. He was the one who stayed through all of it.

The 133rd pick, 11 years on

There is a version of this story where the 1997 trade demand gets honored. Alfredsson goes somewhere else at 25, a fresh start, a real shot at the Cup. This version is about a man who stayed. The Bureau's Book has him at 90 overall, ninth among all 32-year-olds in this league, and the most valuable player on a club that could not win enough games to matter in April.

The draft is 16 days away. Free agency closes 50 days after that. The Senators will draft into a room that needs a center, a second-line defenseman, and a man who can replace the captain if the captain decides the stay has run its course.

$4.70M for a 108-point season at 32 is not a lot of money in a league where Todd Bertuzzi89 earns $2.60M on one year left and Jason Allison82 earns $6.70M at 29 with 91 points. If Alfredsson tests the market, the clubs with room and a missing piece will look. If he re-signs, he does it on his terms, which is what the man who demanded a trade over $1M would do at any age.

The 133rd pick has 66 days. The 96 deke and the 95 pass are still there. Whether that is enough, the Senators will find out before October.
