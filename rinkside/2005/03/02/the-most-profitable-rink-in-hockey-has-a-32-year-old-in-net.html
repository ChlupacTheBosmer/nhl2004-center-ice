The Most Profitable Rink in Hockey Has a 32-Year-Old in Net

New Jersey runs a $38.53M profit on the league's best per-point cost. Its single most expensive asset is a goaltender with four years left and a consistency grade of 50.

Carol BinghamFeatures writer · RinksideWednesday, 2 March 2005
ShareCopy link

Martin Brodeur91 turns 33 in May. He makes $6.70M. He has four years left on the contract. He has never won a Stanley Cup, and the franchise built around him has never won a Stanley Cup either. Between those two facts sits the most profitable hockey operation in the league, built around a man whose own grade book calls him inconsistent.

The New Jersey Devils Devils turn $44.98M in payroll into $80.83M in revenue and $38.53M in profit, the largest margin of any club in the league. Their cost per point, $0.52M, trails only Vancouver Canucks at $0.40M and Edmonton Oilers at $0.45M. They pack 17,300 into the Continental Airlines Arena at $80 a seat. They have 646 prestige points, third in the league behind Nashville Predators and Edmonton Oilers. None of that comes from the box office alone. It comes from being good enough, cheap enough, and in a building big enough to matter.

The largest single line item in the building belongs to a goaltender the League Scouting Bureau has at 92 on the Book today, with a consistency grade of 50.

That last number is the one that caught my eye. Endurance 95. Puck-handling 93. Glove 92. Lateral 92. Speed 90. And consistency, a half grade. The Bureau does not say why. It publishes the number and leaves the interpretation to those who watch. What the watching shows is a man allowing 2.54 goals per 60 minutes on 0.876 saves in 51 games, with 36 wins attached. The consistency grade is an opinion about a pattern, and it is not clear from the number alone which pattern it is. It is the lowest attribute on his card. He is 32.

The room he built

Brodeur was the 20th pick of the 1990 draft. His father was a photographer for the Canadiens. He won the Calder in 1994 and was in the Conference Finals that spring. He has been a New Jersey Devil since before most of the men in his current dressing room were born. The record opens in December 2004 and he is simply already there, which is the only way the record knows a man who has been somewhere a long time.

This season his room looks like this: Scott Gomez82 at $1.00M and 87 points, an expiring contract. Jamie Langenbrunner86 at $2.10M, 70 points in 50 games, one year left. Jeff Friesen85 at $2.80M, 82 points, expiring. Patrik Elias86, 86 on the Book, $4.90M, one year left, and 18 games played. The Devils are paying him $4.90M to have played 29 percent of the season. Their second-highest-paid player has barely been on the ice.

The cheap young core is performing (Gomez at 87 points on $1.00M, Friesen at 82 on $2.80M). The goaltender is performing. The third-best skater on the team is a ghost. Elias is 28 with a year left, and when he has played he produces: 27 points in 19 games across his appearances. 18 games is a hint at a season that never arrived.

Four years is not forever

The contract says four. The Book says 92. The development index says zero, which means no movement. His base grade is 95 and the form has not lifted him above it, nor has it pulled him below. The question is whether four years on the books means four years in the crease, and whether the club's window opens before the crease starts closing.

New Jersey had 87 points as of this morning, sitting behind Dallas Stars in the Presidents' Trophy race but comfortably in the standings. Their previous playoff run reached the third round. They have not lost in the first. The infrastructure is there: 646 prestige, $80.83M in revenue, a building that fills. They do not have a Cup, and they do not have time the way younger clubs do.

Brodeur's save percentage of 0.876 this season is second in the league behind Ed Belfour93 (0.923, in 25 games) and tied with his own number from last year (0.876 in 64 games). He knows what this number means and what it does not. He is 32 with a consistency grade of 50 and a win grade the sheet cannot show me because wins are not a grade, they are a result, and his result is 36 of 51.

What the money buys

The Devils' profit of $38.53M is the league's largest. It is built on revenue that comes from prestige and attendance, which come from wins, which come from a goaltender the Book calls inconsistent. The arithmetic works because the goaltender is not inconsistent. The arithmetic works because $6.70M buys a 2.54 GAA and 36 wins and a save percentage that puts the club one of three in the league.

Brodeur's $6.70M is the highest salary on the Devils' books. He is 32. The contract has four years. If Elias plays 60 games next year at his 18-game pace, that is 83 points on $4.90M. If Gomez is re-signed at something reasonable, and Langenbrunner stays, and Brodeur gives them another season at 2.54, the Devils are a second-round team in a league where only one team advances from the second round to the third. Last year they did it. They have 646 points of prestige and a Cup banner that does not exist.

A town with 646 prestige points and no championship comes back in October, pays $80 a seat, fills 17,300 seats, and watches a goaltender whose grade book calls him unreliable produce the second-best numbers in the league.
