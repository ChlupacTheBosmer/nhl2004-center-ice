The 226th Pick Who Became Nashville's Spine

Four goals in 20 minutes, a flight to the ECHL, eight years in another man's shadow. Tomas Vokoun is 90 on the Book, costs $0.90M, and the Bureau says he will never be steadier than this.

Carol BinghamFeatures writer · RinksideSaturday, 5 March 2005
ShareCopy link

One NHL game. That is all the Montreal Canadiens Canadiens gave him. Twenty minutes against Philadelphia Flyers in 1996-97, four goals, back down to Fredericton. The background sheet records it plainly: not a stellar debut. Most goaltenders who allow four in twenty never make it back to the dressing room at that level. Vokoun did, though it took him another six years and a trip through the ECHL before anyone in the league noticed.

He was the 226th player chosen in the 1994 draft, a man born in Karlovy Vary who spent a year at Poldi Kladno before boarding a plane to Wheeling, West Virginia, and the ECHL's Thunderbirds. From Wheeling to Fredericton, from Fredericton to that one bad night in Montreal, and then to a list. The Nashville Predators Predators claimed him on June 26, 1998, in the Expansion Draft. They had no history, no identity, no franchise record to protect. They needed a body in net, and Vokoun was on the waiver sheet because the Canadiens could not figure out what to do with a Czech seventh-rounder who could not hold a lead.

For four more years he was the back-up to Mike Dunham88. Four years of warm-ups and spot starts, of watching from the bench and going home without a save. Dunham was traded to the New York Rangers in 2002 for a defenseman, a prospect, and a forward. Vokoun inherited the crease by default. Nobody celebrated. The Predators simply needed the minutes covered and he happened to be the man standing there.

What happened next was not loud. He became one of the better goaltenders in the league. In 2003-04 he played 68 games, won 39, posted a 2.88 GAA, and earned his first All-Star selection. Nashville reached the playoffs for the first time in its existence and won a round. The club that had claimed him from a list no one else was reading became the club that owed him everything.

This season he has played 50 games, won 31, allowed a 2.61 GAA, and stopped .873 of the shots he has faced. The Bureau's Book puts him at 90 overall, eighth among goaltenders in a 30-club league. Only Marty Turco94, Roberto Luongo94, Martin Brodeur91, Olaf Kolzig87, Ed Belfour93, Jean-Sebastien Giguere97, and Dominik Hasek86 sit above him. His endurance grade is 92, his rebound control 92, his glove and his blocker both 91 and 90. He is, by the numbers the Bureau prints, a very good goaltender.

And then there is the other number. Consistency: 50. Positioning: 50. Style: 50. Half of the Bureau's scoring categories for a man graded 90 overall read as a passing mark from a coach who cannot make up his mind. Zero shutouts in 50 games. Last season: 2 in 68. The gaps between his best nights and his average ones are there in the column the league tracks, and the Bureau has made its judgment on whether they close.

His potential is 89. His overall is 90. The Bureau does not think he gets better. The Development Index has him at zero form, meaning his 90 today is exactly the grade his base of 91 predicts, and the base itself sits below what the Book had him at in earlier editions. He is a 28-year-old goaltender who has arrived and whose ceiling, by the Bureau's own arithmetic, is roughly his floor.

What $0.90M Buys

Nashville Predators sits at $46.77M in payroll against 84 points, which works out to $0.56M per point. In a 30-club league where the median sits above $0.70M, that is a model season on paper. Nashville carries $22.75M in profit on $66.86M in revenue, draws 15,674 a night at $80 a ticket, and holds 682 prestige points, the most of any club in the league, ahead of New Jersey Devils at 648 and Edmonton Oilers at 610. The Predators have never won a Cup. They have more prestige than every club that has.

None of that arithmetic survives without Vokoun at $0.90M and 2 years left on his deal. A 90-rated goaltender at that price is not a bargain; it is an error in the league's pricing, the kind that lasts exactly as long as a contract permits. When those two years run out, the market for goaltenders does not pay 50-consistency men the way it pays 90-consistency men.

His win total ties the fifth-highest. What separates him from Turco and Brodeur is not the number between the lines; it is the Bureau's belief that he cannot be counted on to do it again next week.

Nashville Predators has played 64 games and sits at 35-19-0. Eighteen left. The room is built around him more than the salary figure suggests: his best teammate is David Legwand88 at 87, the next man up is Scott Walker83 at 82. There is no second 90 in that lineup, no defenseman the Book calls a Norris candidate. Nashville is a goaltender's team in a way that the roster page does not show at first glance.

The Last Two Years

The two seasons between his 39-win year and now are almost identical in shape. Last year: 68 games, 39 wins, 2.88 GAA, 2 shutouts. This year: 50 games, 31 wins, 2.61 GAA, zero shutouts. He is playing fewer minutes and giving up fewer goals per sixty. The shutouts stopped coming. Consistency at 50 does not produce zeroes in that column; it produces 3-2 wins on Tuesday and 4-3 losses on Thursday, and Nashville's last ten games show exactly that pattern: five wins, three losses, two overtime losses, 18 goals for, 20 against. A .900 save percentage team in a five-goal spread over ten nights.

Paul Farkas told The Tunnel's Marcus Bell earlier this month that Nashville is a one-goal team in a tough division. The GM's arithmetic is correct. The margin between a 35-19 club and a 30-24 club is one night in February, and the man responsible for deciding which night it falls on costs less than a fourth-line center in most buildings.

Vokoun is 28. His career before Nashville was one game, a handful of AHL seasons, and the specific humiliation of a goaltender who lets four goals in twenty minutes on a road trip and drives home alone. Eight years later, the Bureau has him eighth in the league, morale at 94, and a contract that runs 2 more years at a salary that would buy a spare fourth-round pick in a fair market.

The question is whether a man with a 50 at consistency can carry a club through four rounds in April, and whether Nashville's front office has a plan for the summer his deal expires that does not require him to be someone the Bureau says he will never become.
