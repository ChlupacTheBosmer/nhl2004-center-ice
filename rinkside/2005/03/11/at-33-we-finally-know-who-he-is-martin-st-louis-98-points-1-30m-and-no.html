At 33, We Finally Know Who He Is: Martin St. Louis, 98 Points, $1.30M, and No Contract for Next Year

Passed over at midget, ignored at college, bought out by Calgary. Now the most valuable asset on a $33.11M profit, and the Book says the climb is over.

Carol BinghamFeatures writer · RinksideFriday, 11 March 2005
ShareCopy link

The first time someone told Martin St. Louis87 he wasn't good enough, he was 15 years old and he had just led his league in scoring. The provincial team in Quebec did not pick him. He went to a junior league in Hawkesbury, Ontario, scored 37 goals and 87 points in 31 games, and then went to college in Vermont.

At Vermont he had 51 points in 33 games as a freshman. He earned interest from NHL clubs in the summer of 1996, after an all-star showing at the NCAA tournament. None of them drafted him. The offers he had been waiting for never arrived.

Only Ottawa gave him a tryout before the 1997-98 season. They released him. He signed with the Cleveland Lumberjacks in the IHL, put up 50 points in 56 games, and Calgary picked him up in February 1998. They sent him to Saint John in the AHL, where he scored 26 points in 25 games and then 20 more in 20 playoff games as Saint John reached the Calder Cup finals.

He made Calgary's roster for 1998-99. He played 13 games. He spent the rest of that year back in Saint John, leading them with 62 points. The following season he got 56 games in the NHL and scored 3 goals. Eighteen points. Then Calgary fired the general manager who had signed him, the new management team decided it did not want him, exposed him in the 2000 Expansion Draft, and when no team took him, bought out his contract.

Tampa Bay picked him up. He made his debut with them on October 6, 2000. Five seasons later he has 98 points in 69 games, a pace of 116 over a full season, and $1.30M on his salary. The contract expires this summer.

The Book says he is fast. Everything else is what he did with it.

The Bureau grades him 96 on speed, 96 on acceleration, 96 on agility, and 97 on heroics. His puck handling is 91. His accuracy is 90. His fight grade is 50. His injury resistance is 51. His ability to separate a man from the puck with his body is 54. He is a 69-inch, 185-pound man from Laval, Quebec, who cannot win a board battle and does not try, and who has scored 41 goals this season anyway.

The Book has him at 87 overall, with a potential grade of 61. The potential number is the Bureau's likelihood of further development, not a ceiling, and it is low because he is 33. The Development Index has his form at zero. He is 20th in the league in scoring at a pace of 116 points, ahead of men the Bureau rates above him.

Last season he scored 43 goals and 122 points in 82 games. This year, with 13 games still to play and Tampa Bay sitting on 92 points in 9th place, he has 98 in 69. The pace is almost identical.

Tampa Bay runs the machine, and he is the cheapest gear in it

The Lightning's payroll is $47.75M. Their revenue is $79.30M. The profit, after the league's per-game model is applied, is $33.11M, second in hockey only to New Jersey. The per-point cost across the whole roster is $0.52M, tied with Nashville for the second-cheapest in the league behind Vancouver's $0.39M.

Within that structure, a man at $1.30M producing 98 points is the single best return the club gets. Tampa Bay has 35 wins, 20 losses, 6 overtime losses, and 13 games left. They are in the playoff picture. Their last ten games read WLLWWWWWWW. They have scored 31 goals in that stretch against 22 allowed, which means the wins are coming in games where the offense is outscoring the defense's mistakes.

He plays with Vincent Lecavalier90, who is out with a leg through April 1 and who has 105 points in 59 games this season. He plays behind Nikolai Khabibulin92, who has 35 wins and a 2.72 GAA. The team around him is the most profitable in the Eastern Conference.

Morale is 100, and the contract runs out in June

The Bureau's morale scale has him at 100, which means a man wants to be exactly where he is. Tampa Bay is not a place that has made him famous. He is not a captain, not a face on a billboard, not the player a general manager builds around on a conference call. He is 26 on the jersey and 20th in the league in scoring and the contract he is on expires in four months.

He was 29 when Tampa Bay picked him up after Calgary threw him away. He was 28 when he scored 3 goals in 56 games for a team that could not be bothered to keep him. He was 26 when he scored 50 points in the IHL for a team called the Cleveland Lumberjacks because no NHL club would give him a second tryout.

Nobody built a path for him. He found each step himself. Tampa Bay is deciding what he is worth for the year after this one, and the rest of the league is watching.

The Book says he will not get better. The form term is flat. His speed, acceleration, and agility are all still 96. His heroics, 97. He cannot be graded for what he does when nobody watches, and what he has done for twenty seasons is what nobody watched: get up, find a rink, find a way to score from inside a body that the league said was too small and too soft to matter. At 33, with 98 points and a pace of 116 and a contract about to end, the answer is that the league was wrong about every single one of them.

The Lightning have 13 games left. He has 98 points.
