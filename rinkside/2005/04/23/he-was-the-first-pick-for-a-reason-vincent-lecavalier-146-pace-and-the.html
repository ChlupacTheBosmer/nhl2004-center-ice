He Was the First Pick for a Reason: Vincent Lecavalier, 146 Pace, and the Run Tampa Bay Almost Missed Without Him

The most profitable club in hockey is in the second round for the first time. Its center scored at the best pace in the league. He only played 59 of 82 games.

Carol BinghamFeatures writer · RinksideSaturday, 23 April 2005
ShareCopy link

Vincent Lecavalier89 sat out 23 games this season. His team finished third in the league anyway. Tonight, when Tampa Bay opens a second-round series against Washington, the question is not whether they can score without him. They proved that by reaching 107 points on a payroll of $48.50M, the 14th-highest in this league. The question is whether they can win with him now that it counts.

Lecavalier's numbers read 105 points in 59 games. That's a pace of 146 over 82, the highest of any player in this league. Nobody else comes close. Jaromir Jagr84 had 145 in 82 games at age 33. Markus Naslund87 and Ilya Kovalchuk92 both finished at 138. Lecavalier's pace would have won the Art Ross by eight points. He wasn't there long enough to try.

What the Development Index says about him is simple: he sits at 90 overall today, with a base of 90 and no movement this edition. He has been a 90 for a while. At 25, seven years removed from being the first pick of the 1998 draft, he is what this club drafted him to be. The problem was never whether he could play. It was everything around him, and everything inside his own body.

Tampa Bay did not make the playoffs last season. The sheet lists their previous run as 0. They spent $48.50M on payroll, roughly what they spend now, and got nothing. No playoff berth, no deep run. Then they went 39-25-7 this year, scored 107 points, and dispatched Boston in six games in the first round. Three of those six came by one goal. One went to overtime. Two ended in shutouts. The series was ugly and it was a win, which is all that counts.

Tampa Bay turned a profit of $40.01M, the largest of any club in this league. Their revenue sits at $93.85M, their attendance at 16,191 per game on an average ticket of $79. They spend less than half of what Detroit spends ($80.89M for 96 points) and they have more points than Detroit. At $0.45M per point, they are tied with New Jersey for the second-best rate in the league, behind only Vancouver at $0.36M.

The first four names on that table are the engine. St. Louis costs $1.30M and produced 114 points. He is 33, he is undrafted, and his contract expires this summer. If he walks in free agency, Tampa Bay loses the second-highest scorer on their roster for nothing. Khabibulin at 92 overall and a 2.62 GAA with 42 wins costs $3.80M with three more years. That is a goaltender worth $7M or $8M in this league. Lecavalier is on a deal that runs beyond next season. Stillman at 91 points has years left. The roster is not about to fracture in July.

That profit comes from good deals, young cores, no panic spending. It is also what the ceiling looks like. When your best player plays 59 games, a profit doesn't advance you past the second round. A center who scores at 146 pace does.

Washington is the problem standing between Tampa Bay and that question being answered in a favorable way. The Capitals finished 104 points, swept Carolina 4-0 in the first round, and did it with a payroll of $55.92M, more than $7M above Tampa Bay's. They beat Ottawa in the final regular-season matchup here and the season series went 2-2-1. Their goaltender, Olaf Kolzig82, played 74 games and posted a 2.79 GAA. His save rate of .869 isn't spectacular, but his 43 wins say he won more games than anyone except Martin Brodeur95.

One wrinkle: Peter Bondra79 is out for the season with a knee. His return date is 2005-05-29. That is a player the sheet has at 81 overall, and Washington loses him for this entire series. His absence doesn't make Washington a lesser team, but it thins a lineup that Jaromir Jagr carries on his own at 145 points. Without Bondra, the second line gets thinner and Kolzig gets fewer days of rest.

They gave up five goals in Game 2 to Boston and lost Game 5 at home, 0-2. Khabibulin answered with shutouts in Game 3 and Game 6. The last ten games before the playoffs read WLWWLWWLWW. That is what $48.50M sometimes buys: a team good enough to reach this point, not always good enough to look comfortable there.

Lecavalier has been a first-round pick's player for seven years. He was the 1998 #1 overall, the franchise center, the man around whom Tampa Bay built everything. In that time they have gone to the playoffs three times that this record can confirm (the sheet shows no previous run in 2003-04, and their Cup total is 0). They have never won a series past the first round. They have never played a Game 7 on the road in a second-round series. Tonight they play the fourth seed in their own building, in front of 16,191 people who pay $79 each and watch a club that makes more money than any other in this sport.

What happens tonight tests whether the money built something that can carry a center through a second round. Whether 59 games at 146 pace means anything if the body fails again in May. Whether $0.45M per point is a strategy or a ceiling. Tampa Bay has answered that question all season long, in the affirmative, every night their payroll went up against teams that spent twice as much. The second round is where the answer gets tested by better people at the other end of the ice.

Lecavalier turns 26 in September. He has one more year after this one before his deal expires, and another year beyond that. The contract gives him time. The window gives him this spring. At 90 overall, at 146 pace, at 59 of 82 games, he has been the best player in this league who was not there. Tonight, if he is in the lineup, the rest of hockey gets to find out what he is when he plays every night that matters.
