They Spent a Year Trying to Trade Him. They Failed. Now He Leads the League.

Washington owned Jaromir Jagr in 2003, hated the price, and could not move him. Two years later he is 33, he has 145 points, and the Capitals are two wins from the second round on a contract that runs six more years.

Carol BinghamFeatures writer · RinksideTuesday, 12 April 2005
ShareCopy link

The piece

In the summer of 2001, the Pittsburgh Penguins traded Jaromir Jagr84 to Washington for Kris Beech, Michal Sivek and Ross Lupaschuk. The Penguins wanted out. The Capitals took the chance. For his first two seasons in the District, Jagr did not crack the top scorers in the league or make an All-Star team. Washington missed the 2002 playoffs entirely. The ownership group, which had spent heavily on veterans and watched the investment dissolve on the ice, began spending its 2003 season doing something else: trying to trade him.

They could not.

Two years later, Jaromir Jagr leads this league in scoring with 145 points in 82 games. He is 33 years old. He wears number 68 for the Washington Capitals Capitals, who are two wins from the second round after a 2-1 and a 4-1 in Carolina. His contract, per the Bureau's Contract Ledger, pays him $11.20M a season and runs six more years.

What the Book says

The League Scouting Bureau grades him 90 overall, exactly at his base of 91, with zero movement on the Development Index. He is the player they expected him to be and no more. His potential sits at 61, which in the Bureau's language means the years ahead are the descending ones.

The per-attribute grades tell the story the overall hides. Deke 99. Speed 98. Acceleration 97. Passing 96. Puckcontrol 96. Balance 95. Those are the numbers of a man who can beat you without touching you. Fight 50. Faceoff 51. He does not go to the corners and he does not win draws. He beats you with the first three.

His morale is 99. He scored 69 goals and handed out 76 assists this season. The ice time is 22.1 minutes a night, the penalty total 27. In the playoffs he has three assists in two games, nothing in the goal column, and 21.0 minutes a shift.

The money problem

Washington's payroll is $55.92M. Jagr's $11.20M is the largest line on it by a distance. The club made $20.44M in profit this season. They sold 16,203 seats a night at $78 a ticket and grossed $84.79M. The revenue is fine. The question is what $11.20M buys when the Book says the player has peaked and the potential is 61 and the fight grade is 50.

It buys 145 points and the league's scoring title, and a 4-seed playoff run that looks, two games in, like it might not stop in round one.

Jagr came to North America at 17, the youngest player on the Czechoslovak national team. He kept a photograph of Ronald Reagan in his school grade book as a protest against Soviet policy, which is why, when the Penguins selected him fifth overall in 1990, he could leave immediately. Two Stanley Cups followed with Pittsburgh, in 1991 and 1992, as a supporting player on a team built around other men. Then came four straight scoring titles from 1998 through 2001 and the Hart Trophy in 1999.

The trade to Washington was supposed to be the next chapter. The failure to move him in 2003 was supposed to be the last one.

What it looks like now

He scored 151 points last season. Eighty-two goals, sixty-nine assists. This year the points dipped six to 145, the goals dipped thirteen to 69, and the assists rose seven to 76. He is passing more. He is shooting less. At 33, with a potential of 61 and a fight grade that reads like a flag, the adjustment is visible in the columns. No more hits along the wall, no more draws won in his own zone. He beats you from the half-wall with a 99 deke and a 98 stride and you will watch him go.

The Capitals sit 2-0 up on Carolina Hurricanes with Game 3 in Raleigh on Tuesday. Jagr has not scored a playoff goal. He has three assists. Robert Lang75 has two goals, Mike Grier76 has one, and Sergei Gonchar78 has been on the ice for two of them. Olaf Kolzig87 has a 1.00 GAA and a .952 save percentage through two games.

Washington's last playoff run ended in round two. They have 104 points in the standings, the fourth seed, and a man who cost $11.20M because nobody else would take him. He is the captain. At 33, he has six more years on the deal, and the room does not want to talk about year six.

The year they tried to move him

The background is public. In 2002-03 Washington finished sixth in the East, beat the first two games of a series against Tampa Bay Lightning and then lost it. The ownership decided the experiment in expensive veterans had failed. They spent most of 2003 trying to trade Jagr. No club took the salary, or the age, or the fight grade, or whatever it was that made the man seem immovable.

He stayed. He scored 151 points. Then 145. The Capitals are in the playoffs with him carrying them. The six years at $11.20M are what happened when a club wanted to let go of something and the market said no.

At the other end of the league, a 20-year-old named Rick Nash83 is +5 on the Development Index with a potential of 95 and 77 overall. The Bureau says he is going somewhere. Jagr is at 90 and going nowhere. Neither number tells you what happened here.
