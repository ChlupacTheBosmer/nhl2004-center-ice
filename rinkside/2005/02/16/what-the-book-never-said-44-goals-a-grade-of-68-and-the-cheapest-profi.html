What the Book Never Said: 44 Goals, a Grade of 68, and the Cheapest Profit in Hockey

Jochen Hecht scores at a 116 pace for the most profitable team in the league. The Bureau's Book has him at 68, the lowest grade of anyone in the top 30.

Carol BinghamFeatures writer · RinksideWednesday, 16 February 2005
ShareCopy link

Jochen Hecht76 has 44 goals. The man with the next-closest goal total on Minnesota Wild has 19. Hecht leads his team in scoring pace by a full 30 games' worth of production, he plays 22.9 minutes a night, and the Bureau's Book grades him at 68. That is the lowest overall of any player in the league's top 30 in points.

The 68 is not a typo or a glitch. The Index has him eleven points below his own base grade of 79. Even the base is low for a man at 82 points on the season. Minnesota runs the cheapest roster in hockey at $29.97M, turns the biggest profit in the league at $37.86M, and does it on a player the Bureau treats like a healthy scratch.

The grade and the shot

Shot power 82. Speed 78. Endurance 77. Those are the numbers that produce 44 goals. Checking 50. Faceoffs 50. Fighting 50. Those are the numbers that pull the overall down. The Book takes all six and averages them toward the middle, and the middle lands at 68.

Put 68 in context. The other player named as Minnesota's best is Manny Fernandez88 at 81 in goal. Wes Walz84 sits at 76 at centre. Hecht's 82 points rank 24th in the league. His 116 pace ranks 26th. No one above him in scoring carries a grade below 75.

Skip over Aulin. Look at the gap between Hecht and Kovalchuk on the sheet: 23 grades. The gap in points is 15. Minnesota found 82 points for $1.10M and the Bureau filed the report as though they had found a depth forward.

From Mannheim, through three trades

Hecht came from Adler Mannheim, a youth team from Baden-Württemberg, the Quebec Pee-Wee tournaments in 1990 and 1991. St. Louis took him 49th overall in 1995. He scored 32 goals and 46 assists across two full seasons with the Blues, and they moved him to Edmonton on July 1, 2001, for Doug Weight and Michel Riesen. A season with the Oilers, then Buffalo for the 31st and 36th picks in the 2002 draft.

Three clubs in six seasons. None of them kept him long enough to build something around the shot.

He arrived in Minnesota before the record opened in December 2004. Nobody knows the date. He has been there long enough to become the player the team is built around, and nobody filed a transaction. His 58 games this season, his 82 points, his 22.9 minutes: these came quietly, in a room where nobody was watching for them.

What Minnesota built around a 68

Minnesota Wild carries a payroll of $29.97M. The next cheapest club is Atlanta Thrashers at $36.79M. Minnesota's revenue is $66.55M and its profit is $37.86M, the largest of any franchise in the league. They cost $0.58M per point. The league average per point among profitable clubs sits above $0.80M.

That arithmetic works because of players the Book underrates. Hecht at 68 scores 44 goals. Michael Nylander78 at 70 carries 79 points as a centre. Filip Kuba78 at 70 puts up 76 points from the blue line. Three players rated at 70 or below carry this team's entire offence.

Minnesota's last ten games read OTLWWLOTLLOTLLLW. Four wins. They sit at 52 points and they are not in the playoff race. It is a machine that turns undervalued players into profit, and Hecht is the cog the machine runs on without admitting it.

The contract year

$1.10M. One year left. Morale is 87, which is fine but nowhere near the 95 or 99 that flags a man expecting to be paid. Hecht turns 28 in June. The contract expires the same month.

Atlanta is learning what this looks like from the other side. Ilya Kovalchuk at 91 on $1.10M will demand money this summer. Minnesota is about to face the same question with a man the Bureau has never respected: what do you pay for 44 goals when your own scouting department says you are paying too much for 82 points?

The answer Minnesota will reach, if the payroll line tells the story, is: not much more. They will look at the 68, at the 50s in checking and faceoffs, and say the grade is the truth and the goals were a hot streak. They will shop him or let him go or re-sign him for something that still looks cheap by every measure except the one on the scoresheet.

The room the grade missed

Hecht plays 22.9 minutes a night on the cheapest roster in the league. His endurance is 77, which the Bureau thinks is good but not great. His shot power of 82 says otherwise: it says a man who can finish from anywhere in the slot, who does not need to create, who only needs the puck and a half-second of separation.

The Development Index has him at -11. That is the most below-base of any player the Index tracks this edition. The fallers list names men at -2 and -3. Hecht is at -11. The Bureau is watching a man score 44 goals and telling itself it is seeing something else.

Maybe it is. Maybe the grade will prove right in April when Minnesota misses the playoffs for the fourth straight year and the 44 goals stop mattering because nobody needed them. That is what the Book does: it weighs everything the player is not, and calls the sum the truth.

The scoreboard weighs one thing only. It says 44.
