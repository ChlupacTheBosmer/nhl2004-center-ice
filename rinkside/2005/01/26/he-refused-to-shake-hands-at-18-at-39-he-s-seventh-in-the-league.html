He Refused to Shake Hands at 18. At 39, He's Seventh in the League.

Mario Lemieux was drafted first overall in 1984 and would not touch the general manager's hand. Twenty-one years later he is scoring at a 158 pace on a 47-point club, and the Development Index still has him below his base.

Carol BinghamFeatures writer · RinksideWednesday, 26 January 2005
ShareCopy link

The handshake never happened. The Pittsburgh Penguins called Mario Lemieux89's name at the 1984 draft, and he did not shake Eddie Johnston's hand. He did not put on the jersey. He told the room in Montreal that Pittsburgh didn't want him badly enough, and he meant it.

Twenty-one years later he is 39 years old, playing center for a club that went 15-23-5 through 49 games, and he is scoring at a 158 pace, seventh in the league. The man who would not accept the Pittsburgh jersey on draft night has worn it through a bankruptcy scare, a relocation threat, and 21 seasons in a building that once drew fewer than 7,000 people a night.

What the Book Says About a 39-Year-Old

The Bureau's current grade for Lemieux is 85. His base is 92. The Development Index has him at minus 1. That means the Bureau reads him as a player slightly below the version of himself that the Book's long-term assessment carries, which is the same thing it read after the 2003-04 season when he put up 150 points in 82 games. The grade has not moved. The pace has.

Last season his pace was 150 on 82 games and 150 points. This season it is 158 on 41 games and 79 points. Fewer games, higher pace. He is playing 19.8 minutes a night, down from 21.8 last year, and producing more per shift. A man who has figured out where to stand so the game comes to him instead of the other way around.

His passing is 96, his decoy 95, his puck handling 92. His hero grade, whatever the Bureau means when it sets that number, is 93. Speed 88, shot power 87. The numbers at the bottom of his sheet read: fighting 50, sprint speed 52, toughness 58. He cannot win a board battle against a 25-year-old anymore, and he stopped trying. He stands at the far hash mark, the defender turns his head for a half second, and the pass arrives before the man remembers where Lemieux is.

The Room Around Him

Pittsburgh Penguins has a payroll of $44.95M and 47 points in the standings. That is $0.96M per point, better than eight clubs in the league spend, but the revenue is $47.21M and the attendance is 14,062 with tickets at $79. The arithmetic of this franchise still runs on the back of one man, and the club knows it. Their best teammate by overall is Johan Hedberg86 at 84. Martin Straka86 sits at 82, Aleksey Morozov79 at 76, the Calder winner from last spring Eric Meloche78 at 74.

This is a team that needs him every night, on every special teams unit, against the other team's top pairing every time the puck drops in his own zone. And he is doing it at 39, on a contract that carries $9.90M and five more years, which means if he keeps the word he made to himself in Ville-Émard at age three, playing hockey with his brothers using kitchen spoons and bottle caps on the living room carpet while his father packed snow on the lawn outside, he will be 44 years old when the money runs out.

The Index and the Pace Do Not Agree

The Index has Lemieux at minus 1. The Book has his base at 92. His overall reads 85. He has played 41 games and scored 37 goals and 42 assists. A 74-goal pace at 39 is not a minus 1 player by any measure the sheet can offer, except the measure of the man at 22, at 26, at 31, who did this same thing with a different body attached.

He missed time in late January with a rib, onset sometime between the 19th and the 22nd, and the return date was the 23rd. Two days in the lineup. That is not the reason his grade sits at 85. The Bureau has been saying minus 1 for a while now. What it means is simple: his sprint speed is 52 and his toughness is 58. At 22 those numbers were different, and the passing was 96 then too. It is still 96.

Twenty-One Years in the Same Building

The Penguins were bankrupt when Lemieux arrived. They were averaging fewer than 7,000 fans at the Civic Arena, less than half the seats, and people talked about moving the club. Johnston signed him to a two-year deal at $600,000 plus a $150,000 bonus. He would not shake Johnston's hand in Montreal. He would not wear the sweater on camera.

Now the building draws 14,062, the ticket is $79, and the club turns a $13.78M profit. That number is not entirely because of him, but nobody at this club is pretending it has nothing to do with him. The man who arrived furious at a franchise that did not want him enough has become the reason a city keeps buying tickets.

He is 39, the contract runs five more years, the team around him has a top-end grade of 84 on a goaltender and the rest below 82, and the Index says minus 1 while the pace says 158. He is going to keep coming back to that building in Pittsburgh, the one his father did not build but where he chose to play anyway, because at age three, with a wooden spoon in his hand and a bottle cap on the carpet, he already knew what this was.

You do not stop doing it because you are tired, or because the people around you are not as fast, or because someone refused to shake your hand at 18. You just stand at the far hash mark, wait for the defender to turn his head, and make the pass.
