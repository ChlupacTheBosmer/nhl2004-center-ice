He Gave Up Four Goals in Twenty Minutes at Twenty Years Old. Eight Years Later He Is 92 on the Book.

Tomas Vokoun was drafted 226th, played in the ECHL, and waited six seasons for a trade he had nothing to do with. Now Nashville is 69 points and he is the cheapest top goaltender in the league.

Carol BinghamFeatures writer · RinksideSunday, 30 January 2005
ShareCopy link

The piece

The one game Tomas Vokoun91 played for the Montreal Canadiens took twenty minutes. Four goals went in. The Flyers put them there, and the young Czech who had come over from Poldi Kladno two years earlier, through the ECHL, through the AHL, through the Fredericton Canadiens' locker room in New Brunswick, stood in the crease and watched them go. Then he went back to the minors.

That was 1996-97. The 226th pick of the 1994 draft, the ninth round, a club's last swing before it forgot about him, had been given twenty minutes of the best hockey in the world and returned four goals for the trouble. The Canadiens let him go. The record from the encyclopaedia is blunt: it was not a stellar debut.

Eight years later the Bureau has him at 92. Third in the league in goals against average at 2.78. On $0.90M. Playing in a city that did not exist as a hockey town when he was drafted, on a payroll of $46.77M that buys 69 points at $0.68M a pop, the sixth-best value in a thirty-club league. Nashville is alive in its second season in the playoff picture, and the man in its net was a backup for four years before anyone gave him the job.

Four years on a bench

The Expansion Draft of 1998 picked him up. Nashville selected Vokoun on June 26, 1998, and the next two seasons he split between the minors and the NHL roster, which meant he did not play. Not because anyone benched him. Because Mike Dunham82 was the starter, and Dunham was good, and there was no room in the crease.

"By 2000, Vokoun established himself in the NHL, though he initially only saw limited action as the back-up to starter Mike Dunham," the record says. That phrasing is polite. In practice, it meant sitting in Nashville for three more years, getting a start every fifth or sixth game, and watching a man two years older than him play sixty games a season in the sweater Vokoun wore in warmups.

Then in 2002, Nashville traded Dunham to the New York Rangers for Marek Židlický, Tomáš Klouček and Rem Murray. A goaltending trade. The kind clubs make when they want a defenceman and can spare the backup. Vokoun had nothing to do with it. He did not demand a trade, did not force a no-move, did not go on record with his frustration. He showed up in October 2002 and the crease was his because the man who had been standing in it had been shipped to Manhattan.

The arcs we prefer have a moment: the call-up, the first goal, the contract signing. But some careers turn on someone else's transaction. Vokoun's turned because Nashville wanted a defenceman.

What the first year looked like

The 2003-04 season was the one that proved the wait had not cost him. 68 games. 39 wins, 29 losses. 2 shutouts. A 2.88 GAA, a 0.866 save percentage. He played 62.2 minutes a night and finished with a Vezina conversation he did not quite win: Olaf Kolzig82 took the trophy that spring with 25 wins in 49 games and a 3.13 GAA in Washington. But Vokoun was in the All-Star Game, the first one of his career, and his goaltending sent Nashville to the playoffs for the first time in the franchise's history.

Nine playoff games, five wins, four losses. A first-round exit that did not feel like a failure. It felt like the beginning of something.

This year: the same number, better company

Forty-two games this season, 26 wins, 16 losses, a 2.78 GAA and the exact same 0.866 save percentage as last year. The save percentage has not moved at all. The goals against is ten hundredths better because Nashville's defence gives him fewer quality chances than it did a year ago, or perhaps because he reads the puck a shade earlier, or perhaps because the difference between 2.88 and 2.78 over forty games is noise dressed as progress.

What is not noise is the 92 on the Book. That puts him tied for fifth among goaltenders, behind Martin Brodeur95 [114] and Marty Turco93 [1018] at 94, Jean-Sebastien Giguere93 [317] and Olaf Kolzig [502] at 93. His base grade is 91 and the Development Index has him flat, form at zero. He is not rising. He is not falling. He is simply at the number the Bureau thinks he should be, which is the most boring thing in hockey and the hardest thing to do.

The contract tells you more than the grade. $0.90M with two years left. Turco is on $0.60M, so Vokoun is not the cheapest, but Turco is 94 and Vokoun is 92, and the market will sort that out eventually. Brodeur is on $6.70M. Ed Belfour [55] is on $6.50M at 39. Sean Burke88 [128] is on $4.20M. The elite goaltenders cost between $3.00M and $7.00M a year. Vokoun costs $0.90M and does not play a point behind any of them.

The room around him

Nashville is 29-14-3-5 through 51 games, 69 points, second in its conference by most measures. The payroll is $46.77M. The revenue is $54.63M and the profit is $17.94M. The attendance is 15,634 per game at $80 a ticket, which is 7th in the league in bodies, and the prestige points sit at 668, the highest in a league that counts 30 clubs.

The best skater on the roster is David Legwand89 [570] at 89 overall. Scott Walker84 [1051] sits second at 84, but he has a concussion and is out until 2005-02-04, and without him the Predators have won two of their last six. Greg Johnson81 [445] at 82, Oleg Tverdovsky81 [1022] at 81, Martin Rucinsky79 [862] at 81. It is not a roster built for a conference final. It is a roster built to be hard to beat, which is what a 2.78 GAA buys you.

The last ten games read LWWWWLLWOTLL, which is not a hot stretch. Twenty-three goals for, twenty-four against. They are drifting, and the gap between a team that makes the second round and a team that makes the first round is usually a goaltender who stays steady while the room wobbles. Vokoun's save percentage has not changed since October. He is doing what he was built to do: not falling apart when the players in front of him do.

The man who went nowhere

There is a version of this career where Vokoun forces a trade in 2000, or 2001, or 2002. He is 24, 25, 26. He has been in North America for five years. He played twenty minutes in the NHL and they gave up on him. Another club would have taken a flyer. He could have been in Phoenix or Pittsburgh or Columbus, backing up someone else on a roster that wanted him to be someone else.

He stayed. He played the games they gave him, four a month, sometimes six. He let Dunham's trade make the decision for him, and when the crease opened in October 2002 he walked into it and never gave it back.

He is 28 now. The potential the Bureau carries is 89, which is below his current 92. They think he has passed the ceiling they saw for him at 22, when they wrote down the grades for the 226th pick and decided he was a backup. The base grade of 91 says the Bureau has changed its mind. The form of zero says he is not about to change it again.

That is fine. He has been fine with less than that for a decade. The 226th pick gave up four goals in twenty minutes, went back to Fredericton, and stayed in Nashville. Eight years later the number is 92 and nobody is asking him to leave.
