At 40, With Four Years and $9.90M Left, Mario Lemieux Is Still Top 14. The Book Says 61.

The first pick of 1984 has one rib injury on record, a contract that runs through his 44th birthday, and a potential grade 27 points below what he is today.

Carol BinghamFeatures writer · RinksideSunday, 11 December 2005
ShareCopy link

In June 1984, the Pittsburgh Penguins Pittsburgh Penguins made Mario Lemieux85 the first overall pick of the amateur draft. He did not shake general manager Eddie Johnston's hand. He did not put on the jersey. He had scored 562 points in 200 games for the Laval Voisins of the QMJHL, told the room he would play for whoever drafted him, then walked out of a contract standoff and said on a camera that Pittsburgh didn't want him badly enough.

Three months later, Johnston signed him to a two-year deal for $600,000 plus a $150,000 signing bonus. The Penguins had declared bankruptcy after the 1974-75 season and drew fewer than 7,000 fans a game at the arena. Over 3,000 people packed that building to watch the draft on a broadcast screen.

Twenty-one years later, Lemieux is 40, in his fourth year on a $9.90M contract, and still in the same city. He has 34 points in 27 games this season, 15 goals and 19 assists, on 22.9 minutes a night. His pace is 103 points over 82, which puts him in the top 14 in the league. The Book has his overall at 88 today, which is 2 below his base of 92 on the Development Index. His potential grade is 61.

That potential number is what the rest of it gets read against. The Bureau puts him 27 grades above what it expects him to finish at. At 40, with four years left on the contract, the Book has already written the ending.

The first season makes the rest look like a coda. Seventy-four goals, 76 assists, 150 points, 21.8 minutes a night. No one has touched that number since.

Last year: 117 in 73 regular-season games, and 11 in 13 in the playoffs. This year: 34 in 27. The gap from 150 to 117 to 103-pace is there in the table. Sergei Fedorov90 leads the league with 25 goals in 29 games for Mighty Ducks of Anaheim. Lemieux has 15 in 27. Markus Naslund89 leads with 45 points for Vancouver Canucks. Lemieux has 34. He is five years older than Fedorov and eight older than Naslund, and he is still in the conversation.

What the grades say

The grades that hold at 40 tell you what he still does. Passing 96. Deception 95. Puck handling 92. Speed 91. Shot power 90. These are the marks of a man who sees plays early and puts the puck where a teammate can reach it. A passing grade of 96 at 40, with 34 points in 27 games, is a player carrying a roster.

What's gone is the body underneath. Sprint speed 52. Toughness 58. Fighting 50. The frame is 76 inches and 225 pounds, and the legs are what the Index is describing when it marks him 2 below base.

The injury record on this save is one entry: a rib issue in January 2005, back after two days. Twenty-one seasons and one short absence. The durability is its own argument, and nobody in Pittsburgh is making it in public.

What Pittsburgh is paying

The Pittsburgh Penguins payroll is $48.92M, with 34 standings points in 27 games and a 9-8-4 record. The per-point cost is $1.44M, third cheapest in this 30-club league, behind Nashville at $1.38M and Edmonton at $1.43M.

That number reads well on a printout. It reads differently when you look at what else the money is buying. The best teammate on the depth chart is goaltender Johan Hedberg86, graded 88. Martin Straka83 has 36 points in 27 games and an overall of 86. The next names are Guillaume Lefebvre75 at 78, Eric Meloche75 at 78, Aleksey Morozov76 at 78. Past Hedberg in net is Sebastien Caron76 at 79.

Lemieux is the most expensive thing in the building and the best player on the roster. Both of those facts are problems when the last year of the contract arrives and the man in it will be 44 with a potential grade of 61 sitting in the Book.

What the Index saw

The Development Index has him at minus 2. Across the league this edition, Vaclav Nedorost83 is up 12, Marian Gaborik99 is up 11, Igor Radulov76 is up 11. Minus 2 on a base of 92 at 40 is the Index saying the same thing the potential grade says in a different voice: he is above what he will finish at, and the slope is down from here. The slope is also slow. He has lost 2 on the Index across the whole edition, not 20.

The room around him doesn't have a second man carrying 22 minutes the way he does. The Pittsburgh crowd of 15,768 watches a team that wins close games, 4 of them in overtime or a shootout this season, and leans on the same man it leaned on 21 years ago. A working-class kid from Ville-Émard in Montreal whose father built a rink on the front lawn, who used wooden kitchen spoons as sticks and bottle caps as pucks before he had real gear. Who scored 562 points in 200 junior games, turned 18, and told a general manager in a television interview that Pittsburgh didn't want him badly enough.

The money is right now. $9.90M a year, four years left. The Book says 61. He is on the ice, top 14 in the league, with a passing grade of 96 and one rib injury in twenty-one years. The man who wouldn't shake hands in 1984 is still holding the stick.
