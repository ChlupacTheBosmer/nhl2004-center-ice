22.3 Minutes a Night at 21: What Tuomo Pihlman Did After the Calder

Same 34 points, half the games, nearly double the ice time. The Calder winner's second season is a different player, and the Book hasn't caught up.

Carol BinghamFeatures writer · RinksideTuesday, 20 December 2005
ShareCopy link

Same 34 points. Half the games. Nearly double the minutes. Tuomo Pihlman78 scored 34 as a rookie last season on 12.7 minutes a night and has 34 again this year with 29 games played and 22.3 minutes a night. He is 21, won the Calder Memorial Trophy in April as the top rookie in the league, and earns $1.05M with 2 years left on his deal. His overall sits at 76. The Book's potential grade sits at 57.

That potential number is where the piece lives. The potential is the Bureau's measure of likelihood of further development, and at 57 the Book does not expect much more from him. His overall is 76. He is 19 points above what the Book thinks he has left to become, and the season is only half over.

[[table:seasons]]

The minutes changed everything

Pihlman was the ninth pick of the 2004 draft, a left wing drafted out of nowhere the record can tell us, and he came into Minnesota Wild and played a bottom-six role at 12.7 minutes a night. He scored 23 goals on 11 assists, which is a volume finisher's line. You give him the puck near the net and he puts it in. His 34 penalty minutes in 73 games say he was a little scrappy at 19, looking for something to grab onto while he found his feet.

This season the minutes have moved to 22.3 and the goals have dropped to 13 in 29 games. The assists have nearly doubled: 21 this year with more than half the season still to play, against 11 across all of last year. He is not finishing chances the way he did. He is making them for other people now. The playing style has inverted, and the points arrived at the same total in half the time. His pace is 96, and he sits behind only Richard Park79 (39 points in 33 games) on the Wild's scoring chart.

A coach handed him a real role, and the shift followed: from finisher to playmaker, from third-line minutes to second-line work. Pihlman has not been the same player for two seasons, and the 34 points in both years is a coincidence that hides a reinvention.

What the Book gives him, and what it takes away

His shot power and toughness are both 85, the top attributes on the Book, and they tell you who he is at 204 pounds and 73 inches: a young man with a heavy shot who will not be moved off the puck. Balance sits at 84, checking at 83, agility at 81. The injury grade is 81, which is why his file shows no injuries in either season, and at 21 that grade has been tested by a full rookie campaign and a first half of a second one with no time on the shelf.

What the Book takes away is speed and fight. His faceoffs are 53, his breakaway speed 52, his fighting 51. He will lose the draw in his own end and he will not deliver a hit that makes a defenceman change his route. Those grades say he is a skill-and-strength player in a body that will never be fast, and the 96 pace says none of it matters when the hands and the shot are there.

The gap between the two numbers

The Development Index has Pihlman at +4, meaning his form reads four points above his base grade of 76. That is the mildest riser's position on the Board. He is not spiking, not falling, just holding the line at a level the Book did not predict.

Among the Under-23s the Bureau tracks, most players with an overall above their potential are rookies carrying a handful of games and a hot streak that pulled a grade up before it settled. Karel Duba77 sits at overall 77 against a potential of 51 in 31 games, but his point total is 14. Fredrik Sillanpaa71 is at overall 77 against potential 56 in 25 games, with 7 points. Pihlman's 29 games and 34 points put him in a different category: a real sample, a trophy in his hands, and a second year that looks nothing like his first.

The Bureau's potential grade will be revised in the next edition. It does not always catch up. It has 30-odd years of precedent to lean on, and most of that precedent involves players whose early grades hold. Pihlman's case is that the grades did not hold, and the 57 looks more like a grade on a different player than the one skating 22.3 minutes for Minnesota right now.

The room around him

Minnesota Wild has 35 points in 33 games, a payroll of $52.07M, and a cost of $1.49M per standings point. Saku Koivu86 is the best player in the building at 84 overall. Pierre-Marc Bouchard81 is 21 with a +9 on the Index and 15 points in 33 games, a first-round pick finding his footing alongside Pihlman in a young core that nobody outside Minnesota is writing about yet. The Wild made the conference finals two years ago and have been in the middle of the standings since, a club that absorbs its draft picks and sends them out on the second line.

Pihlman's morale is 92. The Wild are 9-13-5. He has a Calder Trophy at home in a city where nobody will bother him about it, 2 years left on a $1.05M deal, and a pace of 96. The entry-level money holds for 2 more seasons. Then he will be 23 years old, a trophy holder, and the market will have to tell him what he is worth.
