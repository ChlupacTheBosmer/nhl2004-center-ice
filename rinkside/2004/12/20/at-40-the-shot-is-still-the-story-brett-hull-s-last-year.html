At 40, the shot is still the story: Brett Hull's last year

The oldest player in the league has 27 goals in 31 games, a shot power the Book grades at 98, and 12 months left on his contract.

Carol BinghamFeatures writer · RinksideMonday, 20 December 2004
ShareCopy link

Brett Hull84 is 40 years old. He was born in Belleville, Ontario, in August of 1964, the son of a professional hockey player whose name still opens the conversation whenever the subject turns to goal-scorers. The boy who grew up inside that name was passed over twice by the draft, skated through a junior league in British Columbia, and was finally taken 117th overall by Calgary Flames in 1984.

That is the sentence a scouting report opens with, the one that makes him a curiosity on a depth chart. But curiosity is not why Detroit Red Wings puts him on the first line. Twenty-seven goals in 31 games, a pace of 135, a shot the Book grades at 98, the highest number on his card. That is why.

The pace is the first thing a fan reads and the first thing the fan gets wrong. Thirteen points below last season, at 40 years old, in December. A young man's numbers drop. Hull's have drifted, by 13 points per 82 games, and he has played 31 of them.

The 98

The Book carries 12 grades for Hull and they split into two chapters. The first is everything the sport calls a goal-scorer: SHPW 98, PUCK 92, ACCU 89, HERO 92. The second is everything else: FIGH 50, TOUG 50, INJU 52, ENDU 83.

He cannot fight, cannot take a hit, cannot stay on the ice through a schedule that asks men to play 82 games in the space of a long autumn. The 98, though. The 98 is still there. Twenty years of Book editions, and the shot power has not moved. His overall is 83 today, one notch below his base of 86, and the Development Index shows a form reading of -1. At 40, that is the gentlest slide in the league, and his morale is 97.

The road before the 98

His mother, Joanne, was a professional figure skater. She put him on blades at four years old in the Chicago area, where his father played for the old league's Black Hawks. The family moved back to Canada in 1972 when his father signed with the original Winnipeg Jets. His parents' divorce in 1979 sent Brett and his mother and two of his siblings to Vancouver. The background record notes that he was not close to his father after the breakup, though they spoke periodically.

He was first eligible for the draft in 1982. He was playing in a juvenile league. No team selected him. He joined the Penticton Knights of the British Columbia Junior Hockey League and scored 48 goals in 50 games, and no team selected him again in 1983. The language preserved in his background file says teams were unconvinced of his commitment to the game and his conditioning. He was 6 feet 1, 203 pounds, and he skated like a man who already knew the puck was coming to him.

In 1983-84, the third year of his eligibility, he scored 105 goals in 56 games at Penticton and broke the BCJHL scoring record with 188 points. Calgary Flames drafted him 117th overall.

He turned down the pro contract and took a scholarship to the University of Minnesota-Duluth. His coaches told him the skating had to change, and he listened. In 1985-86, he scored 52 goals as a sophomore, broke the school record, and set single-season marks for power-play goals, hat tricks, and multiple-goal games that still stood when the record turned. He left after two years.

Calgary Flames signed him and put him in the 1986 Stanley Cup Final. Game 3 against Montreal Canadiens. His first shift, he hit the post. He played two games of a five-game loss. Then they sent him to Moncton in the AHL, where he scored 50 goals and 93 points, tying the league's rookie goal record.

One year left

The contract reads $4.20M with one season remaining. He appears on the Bureau's Contract Ledger among the 30 players with one year left, positioned by overall grade among them. His salary is high for a man whose base grade sits at 86, but the contract was signed against the 98, not against the 50s.

Detroit Red Wings carries the highest payroll in the league at $80.89M and sits at 38 points, 120 goals for against 121 against. The last ten games read WWWWLWWOTLOTLL, with a 4-game win streak on the recent end. They are the team that spends the most and sits in the middle. The room around Hull is good: Nicklas Lidstrom91 at 90, Dominik Hasek93 at 92, Brendan Shanahan88 at 87. Detroit has 0 Cups on the record and a prestige of 398, the 14th highest in the league.

The club has been spending against the expectation of a different kind of season. Hull, at $4.20M and 40 and 51 points, is part of that spending, and he is also the cheapest explanation for it, because the man who plays 22.6 minutes and scores 27 goals and cannot fight or take a hit or stay healthy is a man whose value was never on the line for those other things.

What 40 looks like

The oldest man on the top-30 scoring page is Hull at 40. Mario Lemieux86 is 39, still at 55 points in 25 games for Pittsburgh Penguins. Bill Guerin87, 34, leads the league at 73 points with a pace of 181. The league has its share of old men in the scoring column. Hull is the eldest of them.

He sat a game or two in early December. The injury record does not carry a body part, only a return date of December 6, and he was playing again by then. He is playing 22.6 minutes a night because his coach puts him out there, and the coach puts him out there because the 98 is still the best shot on the roster.
