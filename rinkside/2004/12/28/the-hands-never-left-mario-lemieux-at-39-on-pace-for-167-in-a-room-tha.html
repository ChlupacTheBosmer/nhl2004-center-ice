The Hands Never Left: Mario Lemieux at 39, on pace for 167 in a room that lost 5

The Development Index has him one below his base. His fight grade is 50. His passing is 98 and the pace is 167.

Carol BinghamFeatures writer · RinksideTuesday, 28 December 2004
ShareCopy link

Mario Lemieux85 is 39 years old. He has played 29 games this season. He has 59 points. The pace reads 167 over 82, which would be good for a man of 29 and is a lot for a man of 39 who was drafted in 1984 and refused to shake the general manager's hand.

The Bureau's Development Index has him at -1 on the overall. The Book says 87 today against a base of 92. His fight grade is 50. His physical is 60. Those numbers are honest: the body that played 82 games for 150 points last season now asks for 19.3 minutes a night instead of 21.8. The legs are gone. The hands are not.

The grades that remain

Mario Lemieux passes at 98. He dekes at 97. He controls the puck at 94. Those are the numbers of a player who can still see the play before it happens, which is what a man does when the speed leaves and the vision stays. He scored 26 goals in 29 games at 19.3 minutes a night, a rate of 2.03 points per game. The seam is all there is and he has always found it.

The room around him is thin. Pittsburgh Penguins sits at 10-17-5, 31 points, a goal differential of minus 25. Five Penguins are on the injured list, the second-highest count in the league behind Nashville's 8. Martin Straka83, who finished with 117 points last season, has a wrist injury with a return date of 2005-01-27. Aleksey Morozov78, who had 64 points last year, has a knee and won't be back before 2005-01-04. Shane Willis69 has an ankle, out until 2004-12-30. The winger who made Lemieux's last season a 150-point season is in a boot in a room in Pittsburgh or wherever they keep those things.

Without Straka, the second line is a question. Without Morozov, the third is a question. The man at 66 is the only answer the scoreboard has been getting.

Ville-Émard to the Civic Arena

The background sheet says he was born in Montreal in October 1965, the youngest of three boys in a working-class family in the Ville-Émard district. He played hockey at three in the basement with wooden kitchen spoons and bottle caps. His father, an engineer, built a rink on the front lawn. The family legend is that they packed snow onto the living room carpet when it was dark and cold outside.

He went to the Laval Voisins of the QMJHL at 15 and scored 562 points in 200 games over three seasons. When the Pittsburgh Penguins took him first overall in 1984, he did not shake Eddie Johnston's hand. He did not put on the jersey. He said Pittsburgh didn't want him bad enough. The Penguins at that time averaged fewer than 7,000 fans per game, less than half the Civic Arena's capacity. They had declared bankruptcy a decade earlier.

Twenty years later, the Penguins draw 14,161 per game. They made $14.48M in profit last season. They have a payroll of $43.35M. The city that couldn't fill an arena in 1984 now fills one, and Lemieux is the reason.

The contract and the clock

He has $9.90M left on a deal with five years still showing. That is the largest single salary on a team that spends $43.35M in total. The man making a quarter of the payroll is the man on pace for 167 points and the team is 10 wins in 32 games. The money is what the hands earned a long time ago. The minutes are what the legs can give tonight.

The morale line reads 88. Not 100, not 50. Eighty-eight for a man who played 82 games last season, who watched his wrist go bad once and come back, who came back from a neck that kept him out a stretch in December, who came back again in December and came back again the day after Christmas. Three separate absences on the record this month alone. He keeps coming back.

What the Book says at 39

The potential grade is 59. That is the Bureau's way of saying there is nothing left to build. The house is finished. What you see on the ice is what the Book thinks exists, and the Book thinks he is 87 today, one point below where the system thinks his true base sits. A minus-1 at 39 is a Tuesday.

His shot power is 89. His speed is 90. He still gets to the half-wall and the passing still arrives at 98. He can carry a shift, find the open man, and still put the puck in the net. What he cannot do is the FIGH 50 thing: take the hit into the corner and keep it. That was never his game anyway, but now the Book says it plainly, the way it always does when the legs go.

The team scores 130 goals in 35 games, a rate of 3.71 a night. Lemieux's 59 points in 29 games at 2.03 per night means his individual output sits close to what the entire club produces in a single game. The team needs more bodies between the lines and five of them are in street clothes.

The room

Pittsburgh Penguins has lost 4 of their last 6. The last ten games read WLLLLOTLLWLW. Thirty-one points out of 70. The playoffs are a distance they cannot see from here.

A 39-year-old center on pace for 167 points in 19.3 minutes is the story. The team around him is missing its best winger for six more weeks and its second-best for five.

He came from a front lawn in Ville-Émard with bottle caps for pucks. He came to a city that couldn't fill its building and stayed long enough that they filled one. At 39, with a minus-1 on the Index and a fight grade of 50, he is still finding the seam. Five years left on the contract. Twenty-nine games played. 59 points.
