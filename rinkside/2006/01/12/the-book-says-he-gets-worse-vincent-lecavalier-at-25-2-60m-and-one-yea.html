The Book Says He Gets Worse: Vincent Lecavalier at 25, $2.60M, and One Year Left

The first pick of 1998 scored 138 points as a rookie. The Bureau thinks 89 is his ceiling and that he will fall through it.

Carol BinghamFeatures writer · RinksideThursday, 12 January 2006
ShareCopy link

Vincent Lecavalier89 was 18 years old when Art Williams called him the Michael Jordan of hockey. The new owner of the Tampa Bay Lightning stood at the 1998 draft podium in Ottawa, the first overall pick in his hand, and told the room what he had bought. Eight years later Lecavalier is 25, plays on $2.60M with one year left on the contract, and the League Scouting Bureau's Book gives him a potential of 86. His overall is 89.

A potential below the current overall is the Bureau's way of saying it expects a player to lose ground, not hold it. The Bureau looked at a man with 96 acceleration, 96 speed, and 95 agility on its per-attribute grades, and it decided the 89 he carries today is the high-water mark.

The season that started everything

Vincent Lecavalier did not have a normal first season. He came from the Rimouski Océanic, where he won the Michel Bergeron Trophy as the QMJHL's top rookie forward and the RDS Cup as the top rookie overall, and he walked into Tampa Bay and put up 63 goals and 75 assists in 82 games. One hundred thirty-eight points. He averaged 21.3 minutes a night at 22 years old.

The 2003-04 season brought 59 games and 105 points, 47 goals and 58 assists. A leg injury took him out between February 16 and April 2, 2005, and when he came back he carried the same pace through those 59 games. The Tampa Bay Lightning went to the second round that spring.

This season: 42 games, 24 goals, 28 assists, 52 points. A 102 pace. 22.3 minutes a night, which is more than he played as a rookie. The production has fallen by a third since 2003-04, and the minutes went up, so the per-minute rate is down and the ice time is not the issue.

What the speed grades cannot fix

Look at the attributes the Bureau prints. ACCE 96. SPEE 96. AGIL 95. SHPW 95. BALA 93. These are the grades of a man who can separate from a defender at full stride and put the puck where he wants from either circle. They are also the reason Tampa Bay drafted him first.

The other end of the sheet reads FIGH 51. INJU 52. SPBI 52. The Bureau thinks he won't finish a check, can't absorb contact, and won't win a puck battle along the wall. A 51 in fighting on a 25-year-old center expected to carry a first line through 82 games says he is a player the league built for a role he hasn't grown into yet.

The captaincy was stripped before the 2001-02 season. Lightning management decided he was too young. He had been the face of the franchise for three years and they took the letters off him at 21. The background sheet is direct about it: he did not fulfill expectations, and the club decided the weight of a city on a teenager's shoulders was costing them what they paid for him.

What he did in the summer of 2004 was win MVP of the Canadian National Team at the World Cup, a tournament Canada won. The same summer his club decided he was not ready to lead them. Those two things are on the same record and nobody at the desk I work at can reconcile them.

Tampa Bay's arithmetic

The Lightning sit at 53 standings points on a $59.79M payroll. That is $1.13M per point, behind Pittsburgh at $0.91M, Nashville at $0.93M, Dallas at $0.94M, Vancouver at $0.95M, and New Jersey and Atlanta tied at $0.99M. Their attendance is 16907 per game at $81 a ticket. Their prestige sits at 1048, behind Toronto at 1156, New Jersey at 1128, and Edmonton at 1066. They have won nothing.

Lecavalier is $2.60M of that $59.79M. The contract expires in June. Tampa Bay's five expiring contracts total $5.99M, and Lecavalier is the largest single number among them. His morale is 100. He has no injury on the record this season.

His best teammates, as the Book reads them today: Nikolai Khabibulin92 at 92 on $3.80M with two years left, Martin St. Louis87 at 87 on $8.13M with four years left, Cory Stillman85 at 85 on $4.33M with two years, Fredrik Modin81 at 81 on $3.47M with four, Dan Boyle81 at 81 on $2.78M with two.

St. Louis is the expensive one, four years from free agency at $8.13M a year. Tampa Bay could build around St. Louis and Stillman and let the first overall pick walk for a reason no one in Tampa wants to say out loud: that $2.60M was a fair price for a 22-year-old scoring 138 points, and it is not a fair price for a 25-year-old scoring at a 102 pace.

The 102 pace is not the floor

The Book's base grade is 89. The potential is 86. The Development Index has him at zero, meaning the 89 is exactly where the Bureau expects him, not above it. He isn't a riser or a faller. He is exactly where the Book put him and the Book thinks the ground underneath him drops three points.

Lecavalier's situation is different from Todd Bertuzzi89, whose potential of 58 has been absurd for ten years while he carried a 93 overall. Bertuzzi is the case where the ceiling was too low. Lecavalier might be the opposite: the ceiling was accurate, the player found it, and what remains is a slow slide toward the potential.

At 25, a speed-first center with a 51 fighting grade and a 52 injury grade is not aging well on any model the Bureau publishes. The legs that make him 96 on acceleration will not make him 96 at 30. That 86 in the potential column is a forecast, not a ceiling anyone at the Bureau is hoping to clear.

The Lightning have about five months to decide what they think. The trade deadline, what the calendar has as the trade deadline, is 66 days out. Free agency closes 197 days out. If Tampa Bay believes the 89 is the peak and the 86 is where he lands by 28, then $2.60M was cheap and they should let the market find him a new number. If they think the Book is wrong about the ceiling the way it is wrong about Bertuzzi's, they extend, or they trade, or they make whatever choice they think a man who was the most hyped teenager in the league eight years ago deserves now, when on the Bureau's numbers he is already past his best.

Art Williams called him the Michael Jordan of hockey. The Book calls him a 89 with a potential of 86. Both are projections. One was made by a man who wanted to sell tickets in Tampa. The other is made every season by the League Scouting Bureau and revised, like all the Bureau's work, every edition. Neither one told him what to do at 25 with 22.3 minutes a night, 24 goals, and one year left on the paper.
