What a point costs: Minnesota earns $35.27M and wins 16 games

The most profitable club in the league spends less on its roster than it takes home in profit, and its players travel on a budget at zero.

Ruth HalloranBusiness of hockey · The BuildingSunday, 6 February 2005

Minnesota Wild is the only club in the league whose season profit exceeds its season payroll: $35.27M in profit against $29.97M in salary. The New Jersey Devils come closest at $36.14M profit, but they spend $44.98M on players, so their margin runs negative against the roster.

The Wild win 16 games. They sit fourth in the Northwest at 46 points, six ahead of Calgary Flames and 25 behind Vancouver Canucks. They are profitable the way a parking lot is profitable: very few costs attached.

The building does not help, and nothing has been done about it

Minnesota Wild's live facility sum reads -18. Four tracks move a rating in this build. Equipment sits at level 0 (-6), workout at level 1 (-4), practice at level 2 (-2), travel at level 0 (-6). The travel budget is the worst in the league, shared with twelve other clubs at that floor. Every attribute drops by 6 points on the road.

The road record reads 10-15. The home record reads 11-16. Neither is good, and neither is separated from the other by much, which suggests the travel penalty is one weight among many rather than the only one dragging.

The league has filed upgrades for nine clubs since December 4. Minnesota Wild is not among them. No equipment, no workout, no practice rink, no travel budget. A club that clears $35.27M in profit and has not moved a single facility track in two months is running a decision, and the decision is not to spend it on hockey.

What the gate buys, and what it does not

Revenue of $61.78M comes from 13,522 seats at $80 a ticket. That is the 25th-highest attendance figure in the league. The building is not full and the price is not low. The revenue comes anyway because $80 times 13,522 times a season of games adds up to $61.78M whether the club wins or not. Minnesota Wild does not need to be good to be profitable. It needs to exist.

Cost per point reads $0.65M. That places the Wild third-cheapest in the league per point, behind Vancouver Canucks at $0.49M and Mighty Ducks of Anaheim at $0.64M. The comparison flatters them. A cheap roster produces cheap points regardless of quality. The denominator is 46 points.

Morale at 90.9, prestige at 586

The club's average morale reads 90.9, the 11th-lowest in the league. That is below par without being alarming. The individual cases: Mariusz Czerkawski76 sits at 76 morale in 33 games. The rest of the room is unremarkable.

Minnesota Wild holds 586 prestige points, fourth in the league behind Nashville Predators (670), New Jersey Devils (628) and Edmonton Oilers (598). The league's own standing has them in the upper quarter while the record has them in the lower one. A general manager with 586 prestige, $35.27M in profit, and 16 wins has built something the league admires and the standings ignore.

The road and the room

A fellow general manager said it plainly on The Tunnel this month about a travel budget at zero: the modifier is minus six on every attribute on the road, and a young player in that situation "doesn't develop. He survives." Minnesota Wild shares that exact number. Their travel room sits at level 0 with the same -6 penalty, and they have not moved it.

Minnesota Wild will not be bought out of this position by a trade deadline 41 days away. They are not selling, because selling would reduce the asset value that produces 586 prestige. They are not buying, because buying costs payroll and payroll is what makes the profit line work.

The Wild make the most money in the league and will do so at the end of the season. They will win fewer than 30 games. The building holds 13,522 seats at $80 each, and those seats fill whether the men on the ice win or not.
