34 Spells, 500 Days: Tampa Bay Bleeds More Than Any Club, and Its Two Best Men Miss Nothing

The Lightning lead hockey in days lost. Lecavalier and St. Louis played every game.

Doug ReimerAnalytics editor, ex-video coach · The Slot ReportWednesday, 16 February 2005
ShareCopy link

Tampa Bay Lightning carries 34 designated injury spells and 500 days lost this season. No club has more days on the shelf. The Lightning sit third in the Eastern Conference with 74 points and 25 games left.

Pittsburgh Penguins sits second in days lost with 429. Tampa Bay's total exceeds Pittsburgh's by more than a month of roster time, and Pittsburgh is 13th in the East. The league's two most-damaged clubs sit in opposite halves of their conference standings.

The difference is the lineup card.

Vincent Lecavalier90 carries 103 points in 57 games. He was placed on the injured list twice this season. He played 57 games. Tampa Bay Lightning played 57 games. He missed none.

Martin St. Louis87 carries 89 points in 57 games. Two designated spells, 57 appearances, same result. Those two men carried 103 and 89 points from the top two spots on the depth chart. Their absences never reached the lineup card. The club lost 500 days across 24 different names on the roster, and the two men at the top of it never left.

The pattern holds in net. Nikolai Khabibulin92 was hit three times, the most designated spells of any Tampa Bay player. He played 50 of 57 games, an 88% share. The club's other goaltenders combined for 13 appearances.

Tampa Bay Lightning logged 34 spells, the seventh-highest total in the league. Mighty Ducks of Anaheim was designated 41 times, the most of any club, and booked 268 days. That is 7 more spells than Tampa Bay and 232 fewer days. The Lightning were not hit more than the Ducks. When the Lightning were hit, the absence cost more.

Boston Bruins shows the other failure mode: 40 spells, the second-most in hockey, against 335 days. Six more designated absences than Tampa Bay, 165 fewer days on the shelf. Boston's volume reads as shallow, repeated knocks. Tampa Bay's volume reads as fewer absences that ran longer.

What the Damage Buys

The Lightning's payroll is $45.79M. Their $0.62M per point is the fourth-best rate in the league. Their $28.64M in profit is the second-highest in hockey, behind only Minnesota Wild. They carry 496 prestige points, the seventh-highest brand standing.

Tampa Bay spends below the league median, turns a profit the size of most clubs' payroll, and sits third in the East. On every financial metric the front office can control, the club operates at the efficient end. The injury ledger is where that efficiency meets a ceiling: 500 days is not a small tax on a $45.79M roster. The roster absorbed it because the two men at the top stayed on the ice for all 57 nights.

Toronto Maple Leafs is the mirror image of the problem. Toronto logged 50 designated spells, the most in the league, and booked 48 days, the fewest. Fifty injuries that cost the equivalent of one player missing two weeks. Tampa Bay logged 34 that cost the equivalent of three full-season absences. Both clubs are in their conference's top four. Neither model is obviously safer.

The Remaining 25

Tampa Bay Lightning has 25 games left. The standings show 13 points between third and the eighth spot, and the Lightning carry two games in hand over the eighth-place club. The injury cushion is thinner. A club that lost 500 days in 57 games carries no reserve of healthy bodies into the back third of the schedule. Lecavalier and St. Louis have played 57 consecutive appearances between them. Every remaining night in February, March, and into April runs on both men staying on the lineup card. The league booked 34 spells against Tampa Bay before the break. It has no obligation to stop.
