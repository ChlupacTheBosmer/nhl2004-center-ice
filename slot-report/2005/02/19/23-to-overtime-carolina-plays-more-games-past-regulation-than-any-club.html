23 to Overtime: Carolina Plays More Games Past Regulation Than Any Club and Needs Every One

11 wins, 12 losses in the extra period. The 5th seed in the East draws 34 of its 74 points from overtime.

Doug ReimerAnalytics editor, ex-video coach · The Slot ReportSaturday, 19 February 2005
ShareCopy link

Carolina Hurricanes has played 23 games to overtime this season, more than any other club. They went 11-12 in those games. The Panthers sit 5th in the Eastern Conference at 74 points. Strip the extra period and the record drops to 20-17 with 40 points, last place in the East by 8.

This is the follow-up to the overtime-strip method I ran on January 19, when the sample was smaller and the standings less settled. The pattern was there then. It is worse now. Three clubs inside a playoff line depend on overtime for at least a third of their point total, and all three would fall out of the top 8 without it.

Carolina's 23 overtime games come from 11 wins and 12 losses. The point contribution is straightforward: 11 OT wins at 2 points each plus 12 OT losses at 1 point each equals 34 points. The Panthers' regulation record of 20-17 is worth 40. Together they make 74.

Edmonton runs the same trick at 11-7 in overtime for 29 points on top of 48 from regulation. New Jersey adds 25 to their 54 regulation points. All three clubs sit in a playoff position in their conference right now.

The 8th-place line in the East belongs to Buffalo Sabres at 61 points. In the West, St. Louis Blues holds 8th at 59. If you remove every overtime result from all 30 clubs and recompute the standings over the games that actually ended in regulation, here is where those three land:

  • Carolina Hurricanes: 40 points. Dead last in the East. Montreal holds the bottom at 48.
  • Edmonton Oilers: 48 points. 14th in the West. Tied with Calgary.
  • New Jersey Devils: 54 points. 11th in the East. The 8th-place threshold is 61.

Why they go to overtime

A team that goes to overtime at a high rate is a team whose goals-for and goals-against are almost identical over 60 minutes. Carolina's differential is plus 3 over 60 games. Edmonton's is minus 3 over 58. These are teams that are, by goal differential, exactly as good as their opponents.

The bars where goals-for and goals-against look nearly the same height belong to these clubs. Toronto Maple Leafs sits far apart at 299 for and 154 against. Colorado Avalanche scores 231 and allows 185. Carolina scores 217 and allows 214. Edmonton scores 205 and allows 208. The gap between a club's goals-for and goals-against predicts overtime frequency about as well as any single number can.

Carolina appears in 4 of the league's 20 closest season series on the desk's list: against New Jersey Devils (4 games, all one-goal margins, 15 GF and 15 GA), against Tampa Bay Lightning (4 games, 4 one-goal games, 15 GF and 15 GA), against Florida Panthers (4 games, 3 one-goal games), and against Washington Capitals (4 games, 3 one-goal games). Sixteen games against four opponents, twelve of them decided by a single goal. No club sits in more razor-thin series.

New Jersey breaks the pattern

New Jersey's goal differential is plus 22, which should put them above overtime hockey. They still need 10 OT wins and 25 points from the extra period to sit 2nd in the East. Their problem is different: they win enough games in regulation (27) to look like a good team, but 10 of those 42 wins (counting OTW) require extra time. The Devils go to overtime in 15 of 57 games. The 5 losses in overtime cost them a point each that regulation losers do not get.

Martin Brodeur95 [114] started 47 of those 57 games. His morale sits at 93 and his grade in the Book is 93. New Jersey does not need the Devils to be better in overtime. They need him to close games that are already tied, which he does more often than not.

What it means for the standings

The conference tables on this page are point totals, not quality measures. A club that wins 11 overtime games and loses 12 has been good in 5 minutes of hockey, not good over 60 minutes. The standings cannot distinguish these from regulation wins. Both pay 2 points.

Carolina's schedule shows 4 games remaining against teams they have already split in close series: 2 against Tampa Bay Lightning (next on 2005-02-21, 1 remaining), and 1 against Washington Capitals. Those games will very likely go to overtime again. If Carolina wins them, their OT count climbs further. If they lose them, their OTL count climbs.

Edmonton Oilers faces the same problem in reverse. They are 11-7 in overtime, meaning they win 61% of their extra-period games. That is unsustainable over a full season. Their goal differential is minus 3. The formula says they should be losing close games at a rate closer to 50%. The gap between their 61% and a coin flip is roughly 1 point every other game they go to overtime, and 18 of their 58 games have gone to overtime.

Edmonton sits 4th in the West at 77 points, one point behind Vancouver Canucks for 3rd. Without their overtime record, they are a 48-point team tied for last in the conference.

The trade deadline is 28 days away. If the OT record is luck, should you sell now while the standings flatter you? Carolina's 74 points look like a contender. Their 20 regulation wins look like a team 34 points short. The answer depends on whether you believe the extra period is a skill or a coin flip, and the sample says nobody has enough games to settle that.
