Development Index Watch: 5 of 9 Forward Risers Scored Under 23 Points in Half a Season

The Bureau caught every faller it flagged. On the upside, it flags players the scoreboard hasn't caught up to yet.

Doug ReimerAnalytics editor, ex-video coach · The Slot ReportSunday, 30 January 2005
ShareCopy link

The Bureau's Development Index flags 15 skaters as risers this season, their form reading up against the Book's base grade. I took every skater on the riser list with 30 or more games played, 12 in total excluding a goaltender with 3 appearances, and compared their point totals against the flag. Then I did the same with every skater on the faller list with 30 or more games, 5 in total. The question was whether the Index separates production. On the downside it does, perfectly. On the upside, it depends entirely on who you look at.

Kovalchuk has 90 points in 50 games, 5th in the league. Gaborik has 72 in 51, the highest-rated player in the Book at 95 and the biggest riser on the Index at +6. Cammalleri has 33 in 30, a pace the rest of the table can't reach. Nash has 30 in 38 before a hand injury, a pace above 60 over 82. The Index flags these 4 upward and the point column confirms.

Bouchard has 22 points in 51 games at age 20, on a Minnesota roster that carries the lowest payroll in hockey at $29.97M. Nedorost has 15 in 51. Torres has 15 in 50. Aulin has 10 in 51. Radulov has 9 in 51. Five men the Index says are getting better, producing at levels that look like a third or fourth line, the same output a 65-rated winger would post in his sleep.

Every skater on the faller list with 30+ games has 15 points or fewer. The Index flags decline and the scoreboard agrees. Matt Cooke at 4 points in 43 games is the sharpest case: a base-82 center dragged down 6 points by form, producing at a fourth-liner's rate.

The asymmetry is the finding

The Index catches 5 of 5 fallers. It catches 4 of 9 forward risers. A coach reading the Bureau's published Index on Center Ice gets a reliable signal when a player is falling and a coin-flip signal when a player is supposedly rising. The problem sits on the upside, because a +6 next to a 74-rated center's name reads as upward momentum when what it may actually be is a player heading from bad toward mediocre, still below where he has already been.

That is the case for 2 of the 5 underperformers. Bouchard's base grade is 74. His overall reads 71. The Index says +6. He is rising and he is still 3 points below the grade the Book had him at before this season. Raffi Torres carries the same profile: base 74, overall 70, form +5. Below base, flagged upward. The Index measures the slope; it has no view on whether the line has crossed back above base.

The defensemen are the exception

The 3 defensemen on the riser list with 30+ games produced numbers that match their flags. Mike Komisarek76 has 59 points in 52 games from the blue line at Montreal Canadiens, the kind of season that separates a second-pairing man from a first-pairing one. Barret Jackman85 has 25 in 50. Rostislav Klesla83 has 23 in 51. Those are reasonable numbers for their roles and their Index flags.

The Index works on defense because the scoring standards are lower and the gap between a +5 flag and the production it implies is narrower. At center and wing the same flag covers a 90-point season and a 9-point season. Josh Harding80, a goaltender at Washington Capitals, sits at +5 in 3 games played. The Index has no useful opinion about 3 games. Neither should a coach.

I have been writing for 3 weeks that ice time is the most mis-allocated resource in hockey. The Index is the mechanism. Flag a 74-rated center as +6, a coach upgrades him, he scores 22 points in 51 games, and the minutes that would have gone to a proven man go to a form signal the scoreboard hasn't confirmed. The Index is a direction indicator on a road where the next milepost is 12 miles ahead.

Igor Radulov, flagged +5 on the Index, has 9 points in 51 games for Chicago Blackhawks. He will finish the season below 15 points.
