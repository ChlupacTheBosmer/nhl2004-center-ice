The Mis-Slotted Eight: Where the Book Says Ice Time Is Going to the Wrong Player

Rostislav Klesla grades 82 and plays the third pair. Carlo Colaiacovo grades 71 and plays five slots. The 11-point gap is the league's widest, but it isn't the only one.

Doug Reimer
Analytics editor, ex-video coach · The Slot ReportSaturday, 4 December 2004

I've said it before and I'll say it until the data changes my mind: ice time is the most mis-allocated resource in hockey. Not money — you can fix money in the offseason. Not draft picks — those are raw material. Ice time is consumed in real minutes, by real bodies, and once a coach slots a player onto the third pair at 16 minutes a night while a worse-rated player handles five different roles, that gap compounds week after week.

The Bureau's Book gives me the grades. The lineup slots tell me who's getting the work. When a third- or fourth-liner at his position is rated higher than a top-six man, that's a mis-slot. There are 8 of them league-wide right now. One of them is absurd.

The method: I matched every player's current overall grade against his listed slot assignments, then flagged every case where a lower-slot player grades above a higher-slot player at the same position. Grades come from the Book. Slots come from the depth charts. No subjectivity.

The finding: Rostislav Klesla82 of Toronto Maple Leafs grades 82, up 5 on the Development Index from a base of 78. He plays L3LD. That's 1 slot. The third pair, left side. Across from him and beside him in every other role sits Carlo Colaiacovo71, who grades 71 — 11 points lower — and handles 31RD, 42RD, K1RD, L2RD, and P2RD. 5 slots. Third-pair right, fourth-pair right, penalty-kill first unit, second pair, and power-play second unit.

Klesla is producing: 13 points in 25 games. Colaiacovo is being asked to do the work of a top-four defenseman while grading below the player buried on the third pair. This isn't a coaching preference I'd argue with. An 82-rated defenseman playing 1 slot while a 71 plays 5 is a structural error.

The second-widest gap is 6 points, and it appears 3 times.

The MTL case deserves a word. Joe Juneau74 grades 74 and handles 4th-line center, 3rd-line center, and the first power-play unit. Mike Ribeiro68 grades 68 — 6 points lower — and is the first-line center. The better-rated center is on the fourth line and the penalty-kill equivalent of the second line, while the worse-rated one is the team's top pivot. The Development Index has no note on either player this window, so this isn't a form issue. The Book says Juneau is 6 points better. The coach says otherwise.

At New York Islanders, Dave Scatchard76 grades 76, plays L3C, and gets 10.9 minutes a night over 20 games. Justin Papineau70 grades 70 and carries 5 slot assignments. Scatchard's morale sits at 74 — 12th lowest in the league. He knows he's good enough for more.

What I can't quantify from the sheet alone is whether these coaches have information the Book doesn't. Maybe Scatchard's 76 is inflated. Maybe Juneau's faceoff numbers make him a specialist on the PP despite the lower grade. But the Book is the Bureau's published measure, and it's what every front office in this league reads. When the Book says 82 and the coach plays 71 ahead of 82, something is wrong — either with the coach or with the Book. In Klesla's case, the Index agrees with the Book: he's up 5, meaning his form justifies the grade.

Toronto Maple Leafs is the standout because it's not 1 player — it's 2. Both Klesla at 82 and Steve Eminger75 at 75 sit on the third pair while Colaiacovo at 71 handles everything else. That's 2 mis-slots on the same pair, same team, same season. The Maple Leafs are spending $66.97M and have 40 points. They sit 10th in the league in cost-per-point at $1.67M.

The Phoenix Coyotes and Carolina Hurricanes gaps (3 points each) are within noise. A 3-point grade difference on a 50-99 scale isn't an indictment. The 6-point cases are arguable. The 11-point case at Toronto Maple Leafs is not.
